\begin{abstract}
Quantum gravity predicts that spacetime fluctuates stochastically at the Planck
scale --- a ``foam'' that imprints a tiny achromatic arrival-time jitter on light
from distant sources.
We show that this jitter affects first- and second-order temporal coherence,
$g^{(1)}$ and $g^{(2)}$, differently, breaking the Siegert relation
$g^{(2)}(\tau) - 1 = |g^{(1)}(\tau)|^2$ that holds for all Gaussian chaotic sources
when the foam fluctuates on timescales shorter than the optical coherence time.
For spatially extended sources, cross-ray jitter between independent directions
additionally suppresses the zero-lag bunching peak $g^{(2)}(0)$ through a mechanism
that depends only on $\sigma_\ell^2(D)$ and is independent of the foam's temporal dynamics.
The Siegert violation inherits the wavelength dependence of $g^{(1)}$, while
the zero-lag $g^{(2)}(0)<2$ suppression for extended
sources is achromatic and depends on the source angular size in units of the
foam transverse coherence scale; combined with their distance scaling, these
signatures distinguish foam from plasma and turbulence and are complementary
to existing imaging and time-of-flight tests.
Together they open a new observational window on the path-length variance predicted
by the $\alpha$-foam model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A long-standing expectation from the union of quantum mechanics and general
relativity is that the smooth pseudo-Riemannian manifold of classical spacetime
breaks down at the Planck length
$\ell_{\rm P} = \sqrt{\hbar G/c^3} \simeq 1.616\times 10^{-35}$~m.
In Wheeler's original ``spacetime foam'' picture~\cite{Wheeler1955}, quantum
fluctuations of the metric on this scale render the geometry stochastic;
similar conclusions are reached in the Euclidean path-integral
treatment~\cite{Hawking1978} and in modern holographic and entropic-gravity
arguments from very different starting points.
While a complete theory of quantum gravity remains elusive, the question of
whether residual imprints of Planck-scale geometric fluctuations can accumulate
over cosmological baselines and become observable is one of the few empirical
handles on physics in the Planck regime, and it has motivated a substantial
body of phenomenological work over the past two
decades~\cite{Carlip2023,NgPerlman2022}.

A widely studied parametrization, due to Ng and
collaborators~\cite{Ng2001,Ng2003,NgPerlman2022} and referred to here as the
$\alpha$-foam model, assigns the cumulative line-of-sight path-length variance
acquired by light traversing a distance $D$ the power-law form
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_\ell^2(D) \;=\; A_\alpha\, D^{2(1-\alpha)}\, \ell_{\rm P}^{2\alpha},
  \label{eq:NgPerlmanIntro}
\end{equation}
where $A_\alpha$ is a dimensionless coefficient of order unity and the exponent
$\alpha$ encodes the underlying foam dynamics.
Three values are conventionally distinguished.
The ``conservative'' case $\alpha = 1$ follows from Wheeler's original
dimensional argument and gives $\sigma_\ell \sim \ell_{\rm P}$ regardless of
$D$ -- observationally inconsequential.
The ``holographic'' case $\alpha = 2/3$ is motivated by the requirement that
the information content of a region of spacetime saturate the
Bekenstein--Hawking bound and scale with the area of its boundary in Planck
units~\cite{Ng2001}; it predicts $\sigma_\ell \sim (D\ell_{\rm P}^2)^{1/3}$,
of order $10^{-15}$~m over $1$~Gpc.
The ``random-walk'' case $\alpha = 1/2$ treats successive Planck cells as
independent path-length kicks and gives
$\sigma_\ell \sim (D\ell_{\rm P})^{1/2}$, roughly $10^{-5}$~m over the same
distance -- large enough to randomize the optical phase of light from sources
at $z \gtrsim 1$.
The three cases bracket the range investigated most thoroughly; for a recent
review situating them within the broader quantum-gravity phenomenology
program, see Carlip~\cite{Carlip2023}.

Two complementary observational channels have been used to test these models.
The first exploits the fact that random Planck-scale path-length deviations
imprint a stochastic phase $\sigma_\phi = 2\pi\sigma_\ell/\lambda$ across the
aperture of any telescope, reducing the Strehl ratio by
$S \approx \exp(-\sigma_\phi^2)$ and degrading the diffraction-limited
point-spread function.
Detections of crisp, near-diffraction-limited images of distant active galactic
nuclei with the Hubble Space Telescope and ground-based instruments
\cite{LieuHillman2003,Ragazzoni2003,Christiansen2011,Perlman2015},
together with stellar interferometry, exclude $\alpha \lesssim 2/3$ at optical
wavelengths for $D \sim 1$--$10$~Gpc, leaving the holographic and conservative
cases as the only quantitatively viable scalings.
These exclusions assume transverse foam correlations are negligible on aperture
scales; models with long-range transverse correlations can evade blurring
constraints while retaining large $\sigma_\ell(D)$~\cite{LeeZurekChen2024}.
The second channel uses energy-dependent time-of-flight measurements of
high-energy astrophysical transients: gamma-ray bursts observed by
Fermi~\cite{Fermi2009} and TeV blazar flares observed by
H.E.S.S.~\cite{HESS2008} constrain the linear and quadratic dispersion that
some quantum-gravity models predict, with LHAASO observations of
GRB\,221009A~\cite{LHAASO2024} providing the current leading limits.
Together the two channels have winnowed the parameter space but not eliminated
it, and several proposed scalings -- including the holographic $\alpha = 2/3$
-- remain unconstrained at any wavelength accessible to current instrumentation.
A recent analysis of GRB\,221009A claims consistency with holographic foam
blurring~\cite{SteinbringNg2026}, though without formal statistical significance.

Both existing channels share a structural limitation that motivates the
present work.
The imaging tests are essentially binary: a wavefront either remains coherent
enough to image or it does not, so that once a positive detection is registered
the constraint saturates and no further information is recovered about the
magnitude of the residual phase variance.
The time-of-flight tests, conversely, rely on a chromatic dispersion signature
that is precisely what is \emph{not} expected from a geometric-optics
path-length fluctuation, for which the timing offset $\delta t = \Delta\ell/c$
is frequency-independent.
The Fermi, H.E.S.S., and LHAASO analyses specifically seek a Lorentz-invariance-violating
(LIV) dispersion of the form $\delta t \propto E^n/E_{\rm QG}^n$: a deterministic,
energy-ordered delay.
A stochastic, energy-independent geometric jitter produces no such signal, so
the absence of a detected LIV dispersion in GRB or blazar data leaves the
foam entirely unconstrained by those measurements.
A foam model whose accumulated phase variance lies below the imaging threshold
but whose timing jitter is achromatic is therefore invisible to both channels.
Whether observables exist that bridge this gap -- sensitive to a phase variance
too small to erase fringes outright, but achromatic and thus distinguishable
from plasma scintillation or instrumental dispersion -- is a natural question,
and it is the question we address here.

We develop a framework based on the first- and second-order coherence
functions $g^{(1)}(\tau)$ and $g^{(2)}(\tau)$ of light from astrophysical
sources.
The first-order coherence -- the field--field autocorrelation, equivalently
the fringe visibility at delay $\tau$ -- inherits a foam suppression that
interpolates continuously between unity and the Lieu--Hillman fringe-erasure
limit~\cite{LieuHillman2003}, recovering the Strehl and fringe-visibility
tests as limiting cases of the same statistic.
The second-order coherence -- the intensity--intensity autocorrelation
measured in a Hanbury Brown--Twiss experiment~\cite{HBT1956} -- is to leading
order blind to the carrier-phase modulation that suppresses $g^{(1)}$, but
acquires a distinct signature from the differential timing jitter between
photon arrivals.
The two together violate the Siegert relation
$g^{(2)}(\tau) - 1 = |g^{(1)}(\tau)|^2$~\cite{Siegert1943} in a foam-specific
way, providing a discriminant that does not require the intrinsic source
visibility to be modeled.
The Siegert violation requires the foam to fluctuate on timescales shorter than
the optical coherence time; the zero-lag bunching suppression $\delta g^{(2)}$
for extended sources, by contrast, depends only on $\sigma_\ell^2(D)$ and is
independent of the foam's temporal dynamics.
For spatially resolved sources, transverse foam coherence produces an
additional, geometrically distinct effect: a suppression of the zero-lag
HBT bunching peak that depends on the source angular size and provides a direct
probe of the transverse foam coherence length $r_\perp$ -- a degree of freedom
accessible via single-aperture second-order temporal coherence.
Next-generation sensitivity is achievable via two complementary paths:
large-aperture single telescopes measuring the HBT effect, and multi-telescope
intensity interferometer arrays such as
VERITAS~\cite{Abeysekara2020,Acharyya2024} --- both increase collecting area
and improve signal-to-noise for nearby resolved sources, while the temporal
$g^{(1)}$ channel is accessible to high-resolution spectroscopy of distant
quasars.
Detection of both point-like and extended-source signals at the same
redshift would jointly probe $\{\alpha,\sigma_\ell(D),r_\perp\}$: the
point-source Siegert discrepancy is sensitive to $\alpha$ and $D$ with no
sensitivity to $r_\perp$, while the zero-lag bunching suppression for an
extended source is sensitive to $r_\perp$ largely independently of the
intrinsic source spectrum.
Crucially, their consistency provides a test of whether any observed
signatures are jointly consistent with the predictions of the foam model,
rather than a common instrumental or astrophysical systematic.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:model} develops the model: the spatiotemporal covariance of
the foam-induced path-length fluctuation field, the differential-jitter
distribution at a single ray, and its generalization to ray pairs.
Section~\ref{sec:g1} computes the foam suppression of first-order coherence,
derives closed-form results for Lorentzian and Voigt line profiles, and
connects it to the published Strehl and fringe-visibility tests.
Section~\ref{sec:g2} develops the second-order coherence, including the
conditional Siegert relation and its narrow-band limit.
Section~\ref{sec:siegert} quantifies the resulting Siegert violation.
Section~\ref{sec:extended} extends the analysis to spatially resolved sources,
derives the suppression of the zero-lag HBT bunching peak evaluated for
Lorentzian and Voigt profiles, identifies the
distance scaling and achromaticity as discriminants against conventional
propagation phase screens, and shows how detection of both point-like and
extended-source signals would jointly probe $\{\alpha,\sigma_\ell(D),r_\perp\}$.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
% ==============================================================

\subsection{Arrival-Time Jitter as a Time Reparameterization}

We model the detected field as a stochastically time-warped version of the
intrinsic source field:
\begin{equation}
  E_{\rm obs}(t) = E_{\rm src}\!\left(t - \delta t(t)\right),
  \label{eq:Eobs}
\end{equation}
where $E_{\rm src}(t)$ is a single spatial mode of a stationary zero-mean
complex Gaussian process (chaotic light, appropriate for virtually all known
astrophysical sources) and $\delta t(t)$ is the foam-induced
arrival-time jitter, a stationary stochastic process statistically independent
of $E_{\rm src}$.
The jitter arises from cumulative path-length fluctuations along the
line of sight of propagation distance $D$:
\begin{equation}
  \delta t(t) = \frac{1}{c}\,\Delta\ell_{\rm tot}(t).
  \label{eq:jitter}
\end{equation}
In the geometric-optics limit the path-length shift $\Delta\ell_{\rm tot}$
is a purely geometric quantity and hence frequency-independent (achromatic),
in contrast to plasma dispersion which scales as $\lambda^2$.
This achromaticity is a key discriminant between foam and conventional
propagation effects.

\subsection{Spatial and Temporal Fluctuations of Spacetime Foam}
\label{sec:Calpha_origin}

Following Ng and collaborators~\cite{NgPerlman2022,Ng2003,Ng2001} (see also
Ref.~\cite{Carlip2023} for a review of foam models and their observational
constraints), the single-ray path-length variance obeys the power-law scaling
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_\ell^2(D) = A_\alpha\,D^{2(1-\alpha)}\,\ell_{\rm P}^{2\alpha},
  \label{eq:NgPerlmanVar}
\end{equation}
where
$\alpha$ parametrizes the foam model
($\alpha=1$: conservative; $\alpha=2/3$: holographic; $\alpha=1/2$: random walk),
and $A_\alpha$ is a dimensionless coefficient of order unity.

Because foam is a property of spacetime rather than of any preferred direction,
the same dimensional logic that governs longitudinal path-length fluctuations
motivates the existence of a characteristic transverse coherence length $r_\perp$.
However, the longitudinal and transverse cases are geometrically distinct: in the
longitudinal case $D$ counts the number of cells traversed along the ray, while in
the transverse case $b$ is a separation between rays that share cells along the line
of sight --- the number of shared cells depends on both $b$ and $D$ in a way that is
not captured by a simple dimensional analogy.
Accordingly, $r_\perp$ is treated as an independent free parameter in
Section~\ref{sec:extended}.
We denote the normalized transverse coherence function by $\Phi(b/r_\perp)$,
satisfying $\Phi(0)=1$ and $\Phi(b/r_\perp)\to 0$ for $b\gg r_\perp$.
The functional form of $\Phi$ depends on the specific foam model.
For the pixellon model, Lee, Zurek \& Chen~\cite{LeeZurekChen2024} find that
the transverse correlation structure involves long-range angular correlations
(a Legendre polynomial series in aperture-plane angle) that do not reduce to
a simple $\Phi(b/r_\perp)$ ansatz, but whose long-range character strongly
suppresses image blurring relative to the uncorrelated case.
In the present framework $\Phi$ is treated as a free function; a foam
signal would manifest as an angular-size dependence of $\delta g^{(2)}$
(Section~\ref{sec:extended}).

Turning to the temporal evolution of spacetime foam --- an aspect not specified by Eq.~\eqref{eq:NgPerlmanVar} --- quantum foam models posit that each Planck-scale cell along the line of sight fluctuates in time.
Two photons traversing the same cell at coordinate times separated by $\tau$
experience path-length fluctuations that are correlated for $\tau \ll \tau_{\rm foam}$
but decorrelated for $\tau \gg \tau_{\rm foam}$, where $\tau_{\rm foam}$ is the
characteristic temporal fluctuation timescale at each cell.
We introduce $C_\alpha(\tau;D)$ as the normalized \emph{temporal} autocorrelation
of the per-cell path-length fluctuation, integrated over the $\sim D/\ell_{\rm P}$
cells along the line of sight (where $\ell_{\rm P} = \sqrt{\hbar G/c^3}
\simeq 1.616\times10^{-35}$~m is the Planck length).
The timescale $\tau_{\rm foam}$ is an additional free parameter of the foam model,
not fixed by the $\alpha$-model variance relation alone.
If $\tau_{\rm foam}$ is of order the Planck time,
$\ell_{\rm P}/c \approx 5\times10^{-44}$~s, then $C_\alpha(\tau) \approx 0$ for all
accessible lags: the foam is fully decorrelated on any observable timescale, the most
optimistic scenario for detection.
In the opposite \emph{quasi-static} limit $\tau_{\rm foam} \gg D/c$ (foam coherent
across the entire line of sight), $C_\alpha(\tau) \approx 1$ for all $\tau$ of
interest and every temporal observable vanishes.
The parameters $A_\alpha$, $C_\alpha(\tau;D)$, $\tau_{\rm foam}$, $r_\perp$, and
$\Phi_{\rm eff}$ are convenient parameterizations of distinct physical effects ---
single-ray variance, temporal foam dynamics, transverse coherence, and source angular
structure --- and each governs a distinct aspect of the signal's detectability.

The full spatiotemporal covariance between two rays separated by transverse
distance $b$ takes the factorized form
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle \Delta\ell_{\rm tot}(t;\boldsymbol{\theta})\,
              \Delta\ell_{\rm tot}(t+\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta}')\big\rangle
  = \sigma_\ell^2(D)\,C_\alpha(\tau;D)\,
    \Phi\!\left(\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')}{r_\perp}\right),
  \label{eq:pathcov}
\end{equation}
where $b(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}') \simeq D|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}'|$.
The single-ray temporal covariance appropriate for a
point source is the special case
$\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ (so $b=0$):
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle \Delta\ell_{\rm tot}(t)\,
              \Delta\ell_{\rm tot}(t+\tau) \big\rangle
  = \sigma_\ell^2(D)\,C_\alpha(\tau;D).
  \label{eq:pathcov1ray}
\end{equation}
This product form --- separating the total variance from the normalized
spatiotemporal shape --- is a structural assumption, not a consequence of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:NgPerlmanVar}, that defines a tractable class of foam models
within which the analytic framework is exact.
Observationally, factorization predicts that the ratio
$[g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)-1]_{\rm extended}/[g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)-1]_{\rm point}$
equals $\Phi_{\rm eff}$ and is independent of both $\tau$ and $D$; any
dependence of this ratio on lag or source redshift would falsify the
factorization ansatz.
The two principal observables developed below --- the Siegert
discrepancy $\mathcal{S}(\tau)$ and the zero-lag bunching suppression
$\delta g^{(2)}$ --- both descend from the single scaling relation
Eq.~(\ref{eq:NgPerlmanVar}), with Sections~\ref{sec:g1}--\ref{sec:siegert}
probing its longitudinal projection and Section~\ref{sec:extended} its transverse
projection.

\subsection{Differential Jitter and Its Distribution}
\label{sec:differential_jitter}

With the spatiotemporal covariance model in place, we define the key derived quantity
that enters the coherence integrals.
Define the \emph{cross-ray differential jitter} between a photon arriving along
direction $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ at time $t+\tau$ and a photon arriving along
$\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ at time $t$:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}'}(\tau)
  \equiv \delta t(t+\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta}) - \delta t(t;\boldsymbol{\theta}').
  \label{eq:DeltaExt}
\end{equation}
For fixed $D$, stationarity implies the statistics depend only on $\tau$ and the
transverse separation $b(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')=D|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}'|$.
Expanding the square and applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:pathcov}:
\begin{align}
  \sigma_\Delta^2(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')
  &= \frac{1}{c^2}\big\langle
    [\Delta\ell_{\rm tot}(t+\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta})
     - \Delta\ell_{\rm tot}(t;\boldsymbol{\theta}')]^2
    \big\rangle \notag \\
  &= \frac{1}{c^2}\left[
    \sigma_\ell^2 + \sigma_\ell^2
    - 2\sigma_\ell^2\,C_\alpha(\tau)\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{b}{r_\perp}\right)
    \right] \notag \\[4pt]
  &= \frac{2\sigma_\ell^2(D)}{c^2}
    \left[1 - C_\alpha(\tau;D)\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')}{r_\perp}\right)\right].
  \label{eq:diffVarExt}
\end{align}
For $b\gg r_\perp$, $\Phi\to 0$ and the variance becomes
$2\sigma_\ell^2/c^2$ \emph{at all lags, including $\tau=0$} — a new effect
absent in the point-source case, and one that is independent of the temporal
fluctuation structure of the foam (i.e.\ independent of $C_\alpha$).
In the dynamical limit $C_\alpha\to 0$ this result holds for all separations
$b$, independently of $\Phi$.

By the central limit theorem (CLT), $\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}'}(\tau)$
is a sum of contributions from many foam cells and is therefore Gaussian with zero mean:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}'}(\tau)
  \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,\sigma_\Delta^2(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')\right),
  \label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\Delta^2$ is taken from Eq.~\eqref{eq:diffVarExt}.

The Gaussian approximation requires the elementary foam cells to be approximately
independent.
For the cross-ray case the two rays share foam cells along the line of sight, and
independence requires $b \gg r_\perp$.
In the intermediate regime $b \sim r_\perp$ (equivalently $r_\perp \sim D\Delta\theta$),
the cross-ray Gaussian approximation and the $\Phi_{\rm eff}$ approximation of
Section~\ref{sec:PhiEff} break down together; both introduce errors of the same
order and must be assessed jointly.

% ==============================================================
\section{First-Order Coherence}
\label{sec:g1}
% ==============================================================

In Sections~\ref{sec:g1}--\ref{sec:siegert} we restrict to a point source ($b=0$),
for which the cross-ray differential jitter of Eq.~\eqref{eq:DeltaExt}
reduces to the single-ray differential jitter
\begin{equation}
  \Delta(\tau) \equiv \delta t(t+\tau) - \delta t(t),
  \label{eq:DeltaDef}
\end{equation}
with variance obtained from Eq.~\eqref{eq:diffVarExt} at $\Phi(0)=1$:
\begin{align}
  \sigma_\Delta^2(\tau;D)
  &= \frac{2\sigma_\ell^2(D)}{c^2}\big[1 - C_\alpha(\tau;D)\big].
  \label{eq:diffVar}
\end{align}

\subsection{Jitter Convolution Formula for $g^{(1)}$}

The observed first-order coherence is
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) = \frac{\langle E^*_{\rm obs}(t)\,E_{\rm obs}(t+\tau)\rangle}
                                  {\langle|E_{\rm obs}(t)|^2\rangle}.
  \label{eq:g1def}
\end{equation}
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:Eobs} and letting
$u = t - \delta t(t)$, $v = t+\tau - \delta t(t+\tau)$, so that
$v - u = \tau - \Delta(\tau)$, stationarity of $E_{\rm src}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \langle E^*_{\rm src}(u)\,E_{\rm src}(v)\rangle
  = \langle|E_{\rm src}|^2\rangle\,g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau - \Delta(\tau)).
\end{equation}
Since $\delta t$ is independent of $E_{\rm src}$, averaging over the jitter
yields
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)
    = \big\langle g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau - \Delta(\tau))\big\rangle_{\delta t}
    = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathcal{N}(\Delta;0,\sigma_\Delta^2(\tau))\,
      g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau - \Delta)\,d\Delta.
  \label{eq:g1obs}
\end{equation}
The observed first-order coherence is the intrinsic coherence function
convolved with the Gaussian differential-jitter kernel.

\subsection{Closed Forms for Lorentzian and Voigt Sources}

As a representative case admitting a closed-form result, we take a Lorentzian
line profile, which corresponds to an exponential coherence function via the
Wiener--Khinchin theorem:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau) = e^{-|\tau|/\tau_L},
  \label{eq:g1src_lorentz}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_L$ is the coherence time.
Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs} by completing the square and splitting on the
sign of $\tau-\Delta$ gives, for $\tau > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)
  = e^{\sigma_\Delta^2/2\tau_L^2}
    \cdot\frac{1}{2}\left[
      e^{-\tau/\tau_L}\,{\rm erfc}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_\Delta/\tau_L - \tau/\sigma_\Delta}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
      + e^{\tau/\tau_L}\,{\rm erfc}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_\Delta/\tau_L + \tau/\sigma_\Delta}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
    \right].
  \label{eq:g1obs_erfc}
\end{equation}
In the weak-jitter limit $\sigma_\Delta \ll \tau_L$, the Gaussian average of
$e^{-|\tau-\Delta|/\tau_L}$ over $\Delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\Delta^2)$
is dominated by $|\Delta|\ll|\tau|$, so one expands around $\Delta=0$:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) \approx e^{-|\tau|/\tau_L}
  \left[1 + \frac{\sigma_\Delta^2(\tau)}{2\tau_L^2} + \mathcal{O}(\sigma_\Delta^4)\right].
  \label{eq:g1weak}
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma_\Delta(\tau)$ is $\tau$-dependent through $C_\alpha(\tau)$,
and the expansion is in $\sigma_\Delta(\tau)/\tau_L$ at fixed $\tau$.
The correction is positive because $e^{-|\tau|/\tau_L}$ is convex and
Jensen's inequality applies.
The normalization $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(0) = 1$ is preserved exactly.

For a Voigt profile $g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau) = e^{-|\tau|/\tau_L}\,e^{-\tau^2/2\tau_G^2}$
--- a Lorentzian of width $\tau_L$ convolved with a Gaussian of width $\tau_G$ modelling
Doppler and turbulent broadening --- the jitter kernel combines with the source Gaussian
into an effective width and mean,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\sigma_1^2 = \frac{\sigma_\Delta^2\,\tau_G^2}{\sigma_\Delta^2+\tau_G^2},
  \qquad
  \mu_1(\tau) = \frac{\tau_G^2\,\tau}{\sigma_\Delta^2+\tau_G^2},
  \label{eq:voigt_params}
\end{equation}
giving, for $\tau > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)
  = \frac{\tilde\sigma_1}{\sigma_\Delta}\,
    e^{-\tau^2/2(\sigma_\Delta^2+\tau_G^2)}\,
    e^{\tilde\sigma_1^2/2\tau_L^2}\,
    \frac{1}{2}\!\left[
      e^{-\mu_1/\tau_L}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\tilde\sigma_1/\tau_L - \mu_1/\tilde\sigma_1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
      +e^{\mu_1/\tau_L}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\tilde\sigma_1/\tau_L + \mu_1/\tilde\sigma_1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
    \right].
  \label{eq:g1obs_voigt}
\end{equation}
The normalization $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(0)=1$ is preserved: as $\sigma_\Delta\to 0$,
$\tilde\sigma_1/\sigma_\Delta\to 1$, the exponential prefactors approach unity, and
the erfc combination gives 1.
In the limit $\tau_G\to\infty$ (pure Lorentzian), $\tilde\sigma_1\to\sigma_\Delta$ and
$\mu_1\to\tau$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs_voigt} reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs_erfc}.
In the limit $\tau_L\to\infty$ (pure Gaussian), the erfc terms combine to unity and
$g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) = (\tilde\sigma_1/\sigma_\Delta)\,e^{-\tau^2/2(\sigma_\Delta^2+\tau_G^2)}$:
the observed coherence is a Gaussian whose width is broadened in quadrature by the jitter.

\subsection{Narrow-Band Limit}
\label{sec:narrowband}

For a quasi-monochromatic source with center frequency $\omega_0$ and
angular-frequency bandwidth $\Delta\omega \ll \omega_0$, write
$E_{\rm src}(t) = A(t)\,e^{-i\omega_0 t}$
where $A(t)$ varies on the coherence timescale
$\tau_c = 1/\Delta\omega = \lambda^2/(2\pi c\,\Delta\lambda)$.
When $\sigma_\ell/(c\tau_c) \ll 1$, typical realizations of $\delta t$ leave the
envelope unaffected, $A(t-\delta t) \approx A(t)$, and the jitter acts purely as
a carrier phase modulation; the observed field takes the form
\begin{equation}
  E_{\rm obs}(t) \approx A(t)\,e^{-i\omega_0 t}\,e^{i\omega_0\delta t(t)}.
  \label{eq:Eobs_narrowband}
\end{equation}
(Corrections to the envelope approximation are of order $(\sigma_\ell/c\tau_c)^2$;
for $\alpha=1/2$ at $D=1$~Gpc, $\lambda=500$~nm, $\Delta\lambda/\lambda=10^{-3}$
this parameter is $\approx 0.28$, so the exact convolution Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs_erfc}
should be used instead.
For $\alpha=2/3$ the analogous parameter is ${\sim}10^{-11}$
(Table~\ref{tab:comparison}), so Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1narrowband} is exact to any
practical precision in the holographic case.)
Since $A(t)$ and $\delta t(t)$ are independent, the first-order coherence
factorizes.
The jitter contribution is the characteristic function of $\Delta(\tau)$
evaluated at $\omega_0$; since $\Delta(\tau)$ is Gaussian
(Section~\ref{sec:differential_jitter}),
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle e^{i\omega_0\Delta(\tau)}\big\rangle_{\delta t}
  = e^{-\omega_0^2\sigma_\Delta^2(\tau)/2}.
\end{equation}
This holds when $\tau \gg \tau_{\rm foam}$ so that foam-cell contributions are
independent; in the dynamical limit $\tau_{\rm foam} \ll \tau_c$ the condition
is satisfied at all observationally relevant lags.
Substituting $\omega_0 = 2\pi c/\lambda$ and Eq.~\eqref{eq:diffVar}:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)
    = g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)\cdot
      \exp\!\left(-\sigma_\phi^2\big[1 - C_\alpha(\tau;D)\big]\right),
  \label{eq:g1narrowband}
\end{equation}
where we define the rms accumulated phase
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_\phi \equiv \frac{2\pi\,\sigma_\ell(D)}{\lambda}
  = \frac{2\pi\,\sqrt{A_\alpha}\,D^{1-\alpha}\,\ell_{\rm P}^\alpha}{\lambda}.
  \label{eq:sigmaphi}
\end{equation}
We write the foam suppression factor as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(\tau) \equiv e^{-\sigma_\phi^2[1-C_\alpha(\tau;D)]},
  \label{eq:Ffoam}
\end{equation}
so $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) = g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(\tau)$.
At $\tau=0$: $\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(0) = 1$.
At $\tau\to\infty$: $\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(\infty) = e^{-\sigma_\phi^2[1-C_\alpha(\infty;D)]}$,
which equals $e^{-\sigma_\phi^2}$ in the dynamical limit ($C_\alpha\to 0$)
and approaches unity for quasi-static foam ($C_\alpha\approx 1$).

\subsection{Regime Summary}
\label{sec:regime_summary}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs} is always valid (exact within the model assumptions);
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs_erfc} is its closed form for a Lorentzian source and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs_voigt} for a Voigt source, both likewise exact.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1weak} (weak-jitter expansion) requires
$\sigma_\Delta(\tau)/\tau_c \ll 1$ at each lag of interest.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1narrowband} (narrow-band, carrier-phase result) requires
$\sigma_\ell/(c\tau_c) \ll 1$; it captures the dominant physics when
$\sigma_\phi \gtrsim 1$ but $\sigma_\phi \ll \lambda/\Delta\lambda$.
These conditions are compatible: the narrow-band condition controls whether
the amplitude envelope is displaced by the jitter, while the weak-jitter
condition controls whether the carrier-phase suppression is small.
When neither approximation applies (i.e.\ $\sigma_\ell/(c\tau_c) \sim 1$),
the full convolution Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs} must be evaluated.

\subsection{Connection to Fringe Visibility and Strehl Ratio}
\label{sec:connection}
% ==============================================================

The fringe visibility and Strehl ratio are spatial-coherence observables ---
they probe phase variations across an aperture or between two separated
telescopes --- while $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ derived above describes
temporal coherence along a single line of sight.
Nevertheless, both are governed by the same foam suppression factor
$\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ffoam}, because the underlying
phase variance $\sigma_\phi^2$ is common to the temporal and spatial
projections of the full coherence function; this unifies the published
constraints~\cite{LieuHillman2003,NgPerlman2022,Perlman2015,Christiansen2011,Ragazzoni2003}
within a single framework.
Where the temporal $g^{(1)}$ fails --- quasi-static foam ($C_\alpha \approx 1$,
no variation in time) --- the spatial probes succeed; where the spatial probes
fail --- transversely coherent foam ($r_\perp \gg D_{\rm tel}$, no variation
across the aperture) --- the temporal $g^{(1)}$ succeeds.
The failure modes are complements because each probe samples the dimension the
other does not.
In the subsubsections below we adopt the temporal notation while noting the
correspondence to the spatial channel.

\subsubsection{Fringe Visibility and the Lieu--Hillman Condition}

The fringe visibility measured in a stellar interferometer at time delay $\tau$
is $V(\tau) = |g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)|$.
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1narrowband}:
\begin{equation}
  V(\tau) = V_{\rm src}(\tau)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(\tau),
  \label{eq:visibility}
\end{equation}
where $V_{\rm src}(\tau) = |g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)|$ is the intrinsic source
visibility.
Lieu \& Hillman~\cite{LieuHillman2003} argue that fringes are erased when the
accumulated phase randomizes over $[0,2\pi)$, i.e.\ $\sigma_\phi \gtrsim 1$.
In our language this is the limit $\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam} \to 0$:
the foam suppression factor collapses and $V \to 0$.
The Lieu--Hillman condition $\Delta\phi \geq 2\pi$, where
$\Delta\phi \simeq 2\pi\sigma_\ell(D)/\lambda$~\cite{NgPerlman2022}, is
precisely $\sigma_\phi \geq 1$.
Our result extends this binary criterion to a continuous suppression curve
$V(\tau)$ whose shape encodes the temporal decorrelation $C_\alpha(\tau;D)$.

\subsubsection{Strehl Ratio}

The Strehl ratio $S$ — the ratio of the observed PSF peak to the
diffraction-limited peak — is the standard measure of wavefront quality in
telescope optics.
In the Ng--Perlman picture~\cite{NgPerlman2022}, spacetime foam imprints a
spatially varying random phase $\Delta\phi(x,y)$ across the entrance aperture:
each point $(x,y)$ on the mirror receives a ray that has accumulated an
independent random path-length shift from the Planck-scale cells it traversed,
producing a random phase screen with rms value $\sigma_\phi$.
When these phase shifts are spatially uncorrelated across the aperture — the
limit $r_\perp \ll D_{\rm tel}$, where $r_\perp$ is the transverse foam
coherence length and $D_{\rm tel}$ is the aperture diameter — the
Mar\'{e}chal approximation gives
\begin{equation}
  S = e^{-\sigma_\phi^2},
  \label{eq:Strehl}
\end{equation}
valid for $\sigma_\phi \lesssim 1$; for $\sigma_\phi \gg 1$ the PSF peak is
effectively destroyed.

Within the present temporal framework, the same quantity $\sigma_\phi^2$
appears as the large-lag limit of the foam suppression factor.
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ffoam}, $\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(\tau) =
e^{-\sigma_\phi^2[1-C_\alpha(\tau;D)]}$, so in the dynamical limit
$C_\alpha(\infty;D)\to 0$:
\begin{equation}
  S = \mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(\tau\to\infty) = e^{-\sigma_\phi^2}.
  \label{eq:StrehlFoam}
\end{equation}
This equality is an exact result of the Gaussian characteristic function,
independent of the Mar\'{e}chal approximation, but holds only in the
dynamical limit $C_\alpha(\infty;D)\to 0$; for foam that does not
fully decorrelate on any accessible timescale the two sides differ.
The equality holds when both conditions are satisfied: $r_\perp \ll D_{\rm tel}$
(so aperture elements sample independent foam realizations) and
$C_\alpha(\infty;D)\to 0$ (so the single ray fully decorrelates at large lag);
in either limit alone the two expressions differ.
Both involve the same rms phase variance $\sigma_\phi^2 = (2\pi\sigma_\ell/\lambda)^2$.
For quasi-static foam ($C_\alpha\approx 1$), $\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(\infty)\to 1$
consistently with $S\to 1$ in the $r_\perp \gg D_{\rm tel}$ limit discussed below.

It is important to distinguish the role of the transverse coherence function
$\Phi(b/r_\perp)$ from the
aperture-plane phase noise just described.
The quantity $\sigma_\phi^2$ is a single-ray variance: it characterises the
accumulated phase fluctuation along one line of sight and does not by itself
involve correlations between spatially separated rays.
The Maréchal formula Eq.~\eqref{eq:Strehl}, however, additionally requires
that adjacent aperture elements sample \emph{independent} foam realizations,
which is precisely the $r_\perp \ll D_{\rm tel}$ condition already stated.
In the opposite limit, $r_\perp \gg D_{\rm tel}$, the entire aperture sits
within a single coherence patch: the wavefront has a common phase offset that
varies in time but not across the aperture, there is no spatial phase variation,
and $S\to 1$ — the foam is invisible to PSF-based measurements even though
$\sigma_\phi^2$ may be large.
The transverse coherence function $\Phi(b/r_\perp)$ governs a physically
distinct effect — correlations between rays arriving from different sky
directions $\boldsymbol{\theta}\neq\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ — which manifests in
the second-order coherence of extended sources (Section~\ref{sec:extended})
but does not directly enter the single-aperture Strehl ratio.

Both the Strehl and the Lieu--Hillman fringe visibility therefore measure the
same underlying quantity $\sigma_\phi^2 = (2\pi\sigma_\ell/\lambda)^2$.
The difference is observational channel: Strehl and fringes probe the
\emph{spatial} phase variation across the aperture (requiring
$r_\perp \ll D_{\rm tel}$), while the temporal $g^{(1)}$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1narrowband} probes the \emph{temporal} phase variation along
a single ray as a function of lag, with no assumption on $r_\perp$ relative
to the aperture.


% ==============================================================
\section{Second-Order Coherence}
\label{sec:g2}
% ==============================================================

We derive the second-order coherence $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ for a point source,
continuing the framework of Section~\ref{sec:g1}.
The result has the same structure as Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1obs}: $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}-1$
is a convolution of $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}-1$ with the identical Gaussian jitter kernel.
Because the intensity $|E_{\rm obs}|^2$ is blind to the carrier phase
$e^{i\omega_0\delta t}$, foam leaves $g^{(2)}$ nearly unchanged in the narrow-band
limit while strongly suppressing $g^{(1)}$ --- the asymmetry that produces the
Siegert violation developed in Section~\ref{sec:siegert}.

\subsection{Jitter Convolution Formula for $g^{(2)}$}

Fix a realization of the jitter process $\delta t(\cdot)$.
Conditioned on it, $E_{\rm obs}(t)$ is a deterministic time-warp of a
complex Gaussian field, so the Siegert identity holds at each fixed pair
$(t,t+\tau)$ as a purely algebraic Gaussian moment identity, with no
stationarity required.
(Ergodicity of $E_{\rm src}$ allows the conditional time-average to be
replaced by the conditional ensemble average; the subsequent average over
the stationary $\delta t$ process then yields a function of $\tau$ only,
by independence of $E_{\rm src}$ and $\delta t$.)
With $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau\mid\delta t) = g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau-\Delta(\tau))$
and the intrinsic Siegert relation $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}-1 = |g^{(1)}_{\rm src}|^2$:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau\mid\delta t) - 1
  = g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau-\Delta(\tau)) - 1.
\end{equation}
Averaging over the jitter distribution:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) - 1
    = \big\langle g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau-\Delta(\tau))-1\big\rangle_{\delta t}
    = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathcal{N}(\Delta;0,\sigma_\Delta^2(\tau))\,
      \big[g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau-\Delta)-1\big]\,d\Delta.
  \label{eq:g2obs}
\end{equation}
This Gaussian kernel relies on the CLT argument of
Section~\ref{sec:differential_jitter}, which requires $\tau \gg \tau_{\rm foam}$;
for $\tau \lesssim \tau_{\rm foam}$ the kernel may deviate from Gaussian and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obs} should be regarded as approximate in that regime
(see Section~\ref{sec:Calpha_origin} for the definition and limits of $\tau_{\rm foam}$).

\subsection{Narrow-Band Limit: $g^{(2)}$ is Unchanged to Leading Order}

The intensity $I_{\rm obs}(t)=|E_{\rm obs}(t)|^2$ is blind to the carrier phase
$e^{i\omega_0\delta t(t)}$: only the envelope matters.
In the narrow-band limit $\delta t \ll \tau_c$, the jitter does not appreciably
shift the envelope, $A(t-\delta t)\approx A(t)$, so
$I_{\rm obs}(t)\approx|A(t)|^2=I_{\rm src}(t)$ to leading order in $\delta t/\tau_c$.
Consequently:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) = g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau)
  \qquad \text{(leading order in $\delta t/\tau_c$)},
  \label{eq:g2unchanged}
\end{equation}
with corrections of order $(\sigma_\ell/c\tau_c)^2$ from both envelope-timing
and carrier-phase contributions to the intensity.
For $\alpha=1/2$ at $D=1$~Gpc, $\lambda=500$~nm, $\Delta\lambda/\lambda=10^{-3}$:
$\delta t_{\rm rms}/\tau_c \approx 0.28$, so corrections are at the ${\sim}8\%$
level and the exact convolution Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obs} should be used for precision
work.
For $\alpha=2/3$ the expansion parameter is $\sim 10^{-11}$ and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2unchanged} is exact to any practical precision.

By contrast, $g^{(1)}$ \emph{is} sensitive to the carrier phase because
$\sigma_\phi = \omega_0\sigma_\ell/c$ can be large even when $\delta t \ll \tau_c$:
for the same parameters, $\sigma_\phi \approx 280 \gg 1$.

\section{Siegert Relation Violation}
\label{sec:siegert}

The Siegert relation $g^{(2)}(\tau)-1=|g^{(1)}(\tau)|^2$ holds for any
stationary complex Gaussian field and provides a powerful internal consistency
check: once $g^{(1)}$ is known, the expected $g^{(2)}$ is fixed.
As foreshadowed in Section~\ref{sec:g2}, foam violates this relation because
$g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}$ is suppressed by the carrier-phase factor
$\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}$ while $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}$ is not.
This section derives and characterizes the Siegert discrepancy
$\mathcal{S}(\tau) \equiv [g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}-1] - |g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}|^2$.

\subsection{General Bound}

Define the Siegert discrepancy:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}(\tau)
  \equiv \left[g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)-1\right] - \left|g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)\right|^2.
  \label{eq:Sdef}
\end{equation}
Let $X \equiv g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau-\Delta(\tau))$, a complex random variable.
From Eqs.~\eqref{eq:g1obs} and \eqref{eq:g2obs}:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) = \langle X\rangle_{\delta t}, \qquad
  g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) - 1 = \langle |X|^2\rangle_{\delta t}.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}(\tau)
  = \langle |X|^2\rangle - |\langle X\rangle|^2
  \geq 0,
  \label{eq:Svar}
\end{equation}
by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, with equality only when $\Delta(\tau)$ has
zero variance (i.e., at $\tau=0$ or in the absence of foam).
The Siegert relation is violated for all $\tau\neq 0$ whenever foam is present.
This non-negativity holds for a point source; for extended sources the
cross-ray differential jitter can drive $\mathcal{S}(0)<0$
(see Section~\ref{sec:extended}).
The Siegert violation is not a non-classical signature: the foam-modified field
remains a classical stochastic process, and the classical inequalities
$g^{(2)}(0)\geq g^{(2)}(\tau)$ and $g^{(2)}(0)>1$ are preserved; it is only
the Gaussian-statistics identity relating $g^{(2)}$ to $g^{(1)}$ that is broken, due to the stochastic phase accumulated along the propagation path.

\subsection{Sensitivity Scale Decoupling}
\label{sec:scales}

The violation arises because $g^{(1)}$ and $g^{(2)}$ respond to foam on
different scales.
The first-order coherence is controlled by $\sigma_\phi =
2\pi\sigma_\ell(D)/\lambda$, the rms accumulated phase along a single ray;
$g^{(1)}$ is sensitive to it because the carrier phase $e^{i\omega_0\delta t}$
appears directly in the field correlation.
The second-order coherence is controlled by $\sigma_\Delta(\tau)/\tau_c$,
the rms differential jitter scaled by the coherence time; $g^{(2)}$ is
sensitive to it because intensity correlations depend on how much the
jitter \emph{changes} between two detection events, not its absolute value.
These two scales are related by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\sigma_\Delta(\tau)}{\tau_c}
  = \frac{\sqrt{2[1-C_\alpha(\tau)]}\,\sigma_\ell(D)}{c\tau_c}
  = \sigma_\phi \cdot \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda}\cdot\sqrt{2[1-C_\alpha(\tau)]},
  \label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
using $\omega_0\tau_c = \lambda/\Delta\lambda$.
The factor $\Delta\lambda/\lambda \ll 1$ means that even when $\sigma_\phi \gg 1$
(fringes erased), $\sigma_\Delta(\tau)/\tau_c$ can be small — so $g^{(2)}$ remains
near its intrinsic value while $g^{(1)}$ is suppressed.
This decoupling is the physical root of the Siegert violation.
Numerical values of both scales are given in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}
(evaluated at the dynamical limit $C_\alpha\to 0$; for quasi-static foam
$C_\alpha\approx 1$ the ratio vanishes at all lags).

\subsection{Explicit Form and Properties in the Narrow-Band Limit}

In the narrow-band limit, $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs} \approx g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2unchanged}), while $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}$ is suppressed by
$\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}(\tau)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1narrowband}).
Using the intrinsic Siegert relation $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau)-1=|g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)|^2$,
to leading order:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}(\tau) \approx
  |g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)|^2\!\left(1-e^{-2\sigma_\phi^2[1-C_\alpha(\tau;D)]}\right).
  \label{eq:Sexplicit}
\end{equation}
In the weak-jitter limit $\sigma_\phi\ll 1$,
$\mathcal{S}\approx 2\sigma_\phi^2[1-C_\alpha]\,|g^{(1)}_{\rm src}|^2$ — linear
in $\sigma_\phi^2$ and small.
In the strong-jitter limit $\sigma_\phi\gg 1$, the exponential vanishes and
$\mathcal{S}\approx|g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)|^2$: $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}-1$ retains
its intrinsic value while $|g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}|^2\to 0$, producing a full violation.
Quasi-static foam ($C_\alpha\approx 1$) gives $\mathcal{S}\approx 0$ at all lags
— foam is invisible to all coherence observables in this limit.
The discrepancy $\mathcal{S}(\tau)$ is a direct observable — both
$g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}$ and $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}$ are measurable from the same
dataset — and the modulation beyond the prefactor $|g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)|^2$
is controlled solely by $\alpha$, $D$, and $C_\alpha(\tau;D)$, independently
of the unknown source spectrum.
For a Lorentzian source, $|g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau_L)|^2 = e^{-2}$, and in the
dynamical limit $C_\alpha\to 0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sexplicit} reduces to
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}(\tau_L) = e^{-2}\!\left(1-e^{-2\sigma_\phi^2}\right),
  \label{eq:Stc}
\end{equation}
the detection observable at lag $\tau_L$.
In the weak-foam limit $\sigma_\phi\ll 1$ this is $\approx 2e^{-2}\sigma_\phi^2$;
in the strong-foam limit $\sigma_\phi\gg 1$ it saturates at $e^{-2}\approx 0.135$.
Although derived here for a point source, Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sexplicit} applies
equally to extended sources at finite lag $\tau\neq 0$:
$g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ is unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:extended}), and the
additional cross-ray jitter that modifies $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}$ at $\tau=0$
produces only $O((\sigma_{\rm eff}/\tau_c)^2)$ corrections at finite lag in the
narrow-band limit.
The extended-source novelty --- the linear-in-$s$ bunching suppression
$\delta g^{(2)}(0)$ --- is confined to zero lag, where the cusp in
$g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$ amplifies the cross-ray differential jitter into a
first-order effect.

% ==============================================================
\section{Extended Sources: Transverse Foam Coherence}
\label{sec:extended}
% ==============================================================

We now generalize from a point source to an extended incoherent source.
The first-order coherence $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}$ turns out to be unchanged from the
point-source result, because $g^{(1)}$ probes only single-ray statistics.
The second-order coherence $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}$, however, acquires new structure
through cross-ray differential jitter between rays arriving from different sky
directions, producing a suppression of the zero-lag bunching peak that directly
probes the transverse foam coherence length $r_\perp$.

\subsection{Field Model and Spatiotemporal Covariance}

We generalize to an extended incoherent source whose angular brightness
distribution is $I(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ (normalized to unity).
The analysis assumes a point (single spatial mode) detector; a finite aperture
would introduce Van~Cittert--Zernike geometric phases that modify the angular
coherence.
The detected field is a weighted superposition over rays labelled by direction
offset $\boldsymbol{\theta}$:
\begin{equation}
  E_{\rm obs}(t) = \int d\boldsymbol{\theta}\,I^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,
  E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\!\left(t - \delta t(t;\boldsymbol{\theta})\right),
  \label{eq:EobsExt}
\end{equation}
where the $E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ are independent, identically distributed
complex Gaussian processes (one per sky direction), and each ray carries its
own foam jitter $\delta t(t;\boldsymbol{\theta})$.
The spatiotemporal covariance of the path-length aggregate is given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pathcov} of Section~\ref{sec:model}, with
$b(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}') \simeq D|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}'|$
the transverse separation between rays evaluated at the source end.
For foam distributed uniformly along the line of sight the ray separation at
position $z$ is $z|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}'|$, so the full
cross-covariance integral involves an average over $0\leq z\leq D$;
setting $b = D|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}'|$ evaluates the separation
at the source plane.
Because $r_\perp$ is treated as an effective free parameter, the $O(1)$ geometric
factor from the $z$-integral is absorbed into its definition; the angular-size
dependence of $\delta g^{(2)}$ derived in Section~\ref{sec:PhiEff} is the
corresponding observational signature.

\subsection{First-Order Coherence is Unchanged}

The numerator of $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ involves cross terms
$\langle E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\ldots)\,E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}'}(\ldots)\rangle$.
Because different source directions are spatially incoherent,
\begin{equation}
  \langle E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)\,E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}'}(s')\rangle
  \propto \delta(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}'),
\end{equation}
so only the diagonal $\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ contributes.
Each diagonal term reproduces the single-ray result of
Section~\ref{sec:narrowband}, and the angular integral over $I$ gives unity.
Therefore:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)
  = g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)\cdot e^{-\sigma_\phi^2[1-C_\alpha(\tau;D)]},
  \label{eq:g1ext}
\end{equation}
identical to the point-source result Eq.~\eqref{eq:g1narrowband}.
The first-order coherence probes only single-ray statistics; the transverse
foam structure leaves no imprint on $g^{(1)}$.

\subsection{Second-Order Coherence for an Extended Source}

The unnormalized intensity correlator is
$\langle I(t)I(t+\tau)\rangle = \langle E^*(t)E(t)E^*(t+\tau)E(t+\tau)\rangle$,
where $E \equiv E_{\rm obs}$ is the field of Eq.~\eqref{eq:EobsExt}.
Expanding the double angular integral and applying the Wick (moment) theorem
to the four-point function of the zero-mean complex Gaussian field
$E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ produces two pairings (the $I^{1/2}$ weight factors
enter the angular integral and are suppressed here for clarity):
\begin{align}
  &\langle E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_1}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_2}
            E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_3}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_4}\rangle
  =
  \langle E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_1}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_2}\rangle
  \langle E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_3}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_4}\rangle
  +
  \langle E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_1}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_4}\rangle
  \langle E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_3}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_2}\rangle.
  \label{eq:Wick}
\end{align}
The first pairing contributes $\langle I(t)\rangle\langle I(t+\tau)\rangle$
(the disconnected term, which cancels when forming $g^{(2)}-1$).
The second pairing gives the connected contribution.
Because different source directions are spatially incoherent,
$\langle E^*_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)\,E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}'}(s')\rangle
\propto \delta(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}')$,
so the connected pairing requires $\boldsymbol{\theta}_1=\boldsymbol{\theta}_4$
and $\boldsymbol{\theta}_3=\boldsymbol{\theta}_2$.
The four-fold angular integral therefore collapses to a double integral
over ray pairs $(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')$, each pair
contributing its own jitter-convolved $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) - 1
  = \int\!\!\int d\boldsymbol{\theta}\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}'\;
    I(\boldsymbol{\theta})I(\boldsymbol{\theta}')
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
      \mathcal{N}\!\left(\Delta;0,\sigma_\Delta^2(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')\right)\,
      \big[g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau-\Delta)-1\big]\,d\Delta,
  \label{eq:g2obsExt}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\Delta^2(\tau;\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')$ is from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:diffVarExt} (Section~\ref{sec:differential_jitter}).
The key new feature relative to the point-source case is that for $b\gg r_\perp$,
$\Phi\to 0$ and $\sigma_\Delta^2\to 2\sigma_\ell^2/c^2$ at all lags, including
$\tau=0$.
In the limit $\Phi\equiv 1$ (all rays transversely coherent),
every kernel reduces to the single-ray $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\Delta^2(\tau))$ and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obsExt} reduces to the point-source result Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obs}.

\subsection{Effective Transverse Coherence Factor}
\label{sec:PhiEff}

To reduce Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obsExt} to a tractable form, define the
effective transverse coherence factor:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{\rm eff}
  \equiv \int\!\!\int d\boldsymbol{\theta}\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}'\;
         I(\boldsymbol{\theta})I(\boldsymbol{\theta}')\,
         \Phi\!\left(\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}')}{r_\perp}\right).
  \label{eq:PhiEff}
\end{equation}
This replaces the ray-dependent differential variance with a single effective
variance:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\tau,\rm eff}^2(\tau)
  \equiv \frac{2\sigma_\ell^2(D)}{c^2}
         \Big[1 - C_\alpha(\tau;D)\,\Phi_{\rm eff}\Big],
  \label{eq:sigmaEff}
\end{equation}
and allows Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obsExt} to be approximated by the simpler
1D convolution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obs} with $\sigma_\Delta^2$ replaced by
$\sigma_{\tau,\rm eff}^2$.
The two limits are:
$\Phi_{\rm eff}\to 1$ (point-like source or $r_\perp\gg D\,\Delta\theta$)
recovers the single-ray result; $\Phi_{\rm eff}\to 0$ (fully incoherent,
$r_\perp\ll D\,\Delta\theta$) maximizes the effective variance.
The approximation becomes exact in both limits.
At intermediate angular sizes ($r_\perp \sim D\,\Delta\theta$), $\Phi(b/r_\perp)$
varies across ray pairs and the $\Phi_{\rm eff}$ reduction introduces errors
that have not been quantified here; they must be assessed jointly with the CLT
breakdown of Section~\ref{sec:differential_jitter}, since both occur in the
same regime $b\sim r_\perp$, and we leave a worked numerical comparison to future work.

\subsection{Extended-Source Siegert Discrepancy and Zero-Lag Bunching}
\label{sec:zerolag}

For the extended source, $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$ is unchanged from the
point-source result (Eq.~\ref{eq:g1ext}), while $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$
is given by the general mixture Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obsExt}.
Unlike the point-source case, in the narrow-band limit $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}$
is \emph{not} equal to $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$: the cross-ray differential jitter
modifies the intensity correlation through the envelope timing, even though
the carrier phase does not affect individual intensities.
The Siegert discrepancy is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}(\tau)
  = \left[g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) - 1\right]
    - \left|g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)\right|^2,
  \label{eq:SextDef}
\end{equation}
where $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)-1$ is given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:g2obsExt} and
$|g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)|^2 = |g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)|^2\,e^{-2\sigma_\phi^2[1-C_\alpha(\tau)]}$.
At finite lag the cross-ray jitter correction to $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}$ is
$O((\sigma_{\rm eff}/\tau_c)^2)$ in the narrow-band limit, and
$\mathcal{S}(\tau\neq 0)$ reduces to the point-source result
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sexplicit}; the remainder of this subsection focuses on $\tau=0$,
where the cusp produces a qualitatively distinct signature.

At $\tau=0$, $g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(0)=1$ exactly by the spatial-incoherence argument of
Section~\ref{sec:extended}, so $\mathcal{S}(0) = g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0) - 2$.
Evaluating the zero-lag effective variance with $C_\alpha(0;D)=1$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_0^2
  \equiv \frac{2\sigma_\ell^2(D)}{c^2}(1-\Phi_{\rm eff}),
  \label{eq:sigma0}
\end{equation}
which is strictly positive whenever $\Phi_{\rm eff}<1$, so the ray-pair mixture
reduces to a single Gaussian kernel of width $\sigma_0$.

For a Lorentzian source, $g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau) = e^{-|\tau|/\tau_L}$,
so the Siegert relation gives $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau) - 1 = e^{-2|\tau|/\tau_L}$.
The zero-lag convolution over $\Delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_0^2)$
then becomes, setting $s \equiv \sigma_0/\tau_L$ and splitting on the sign
of $\Delta$ (both halves contributing equally by symmetry of $|\Delta|$),
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle e^{-2|\Delta|/\tau_L}\big\rangle
  = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_0}\int_0^\infty
    e^{-2\Delta/\tau_L}e^{-\Delta^2/2\sigma_0^2}d\Delta
  = e^{2s^2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\sqrt{2}\,s\right),
  \label{eq:zerolagavg}
\end{equation}
which equals $1$ as $s\to 0$ (no suppression) and $0$ as $s\to\infty$
via the standard asymptotic $e^{x^2}\mathrm{erfc}(x)\sim 1/(x\sqrt{\pi})$
(full suppression, $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0)\to 1$). So
\begin{equation}
  g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0)
    = 1 + e^{2s^2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\sqrt{2}\,s\right),
    \qquad s\equiv\sigma_0/\tau_L,
  \label{eq:g2zero}
\end{equation}
and the zero-lag bunching peak is suppressed below its ideal value of 2 by
\begin{equation}
  \delta g^{(2)}
  \equiv 2 - g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0)
  = 1 - e^{2s^2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\sqrt{2}\,s\right),
  \label{eq:deltag2}
\end{equation}
In the small-$s$ limit, $\delta g^{(2)} \approx 2\sqrt{2/\pi}\,s + O(s^2)$,
so the suppression is linear in $s$ for a Lorentzian source.
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}(0) = -\,\delta g^{(2)} \leq 0.
  \label{eq:S0ext}
\end{equation}
This sign is qualitatively distinct from the point-source result
$\mathcal{S}(\tau\neq 0)\geq 0$ of Section~\ref{sec:siegert}: a negative
zero-lag Siegert discrepancy is therefore a sign-flip diagnostic that
distinguishes spatially resolved from unresolved foam signatures.
In the point-source limit $\Phi_{\rm eff}\to 1$, $\sigma_0\to 0$,
$\delta g^{(2)}\to 0$, and $\mathcal{S}(0)\to 0$, recovering the point-source result.
Since $\sigma_0^2\propto(1-\Phi_{\rm eff})$ and $\Phi_{\rm eff}$ decreases
as the source angular diameter increases at fixed $D$ and $r_\perp$, more
extended sources experience greater suppression of the bunching peak and a
more negative Siegert discrepancy at zero lag.

For an astrophysically more representative Voigt profile --- a Lorentzian of
width $\tau_L$ convolved with a Gaussian of width $\tau_G$ (modelling natural
plus Doppler/turbulent broadening) --- the coherence function is
$g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau) = e^{-|\tau|/\tau_L}\,e^{-\tau^2/2\tau_G^2}$,
giving $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau)-1 = e^{-2|\tau|/\tau_L}\,e^{-\tau^2/\tau_G^2}$.
The zero-lag convolution over $\Delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_0^2)$ combines the
source Gaussian with the jitter kernel into an effective width
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma_0^2\,\tau_G^2}{2\sigma_0^2+\tau_G^2},
  \label{eq:sigmatilde}
\end{equation}
yielding
\begin{equation}
  g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0) - 1
  = \frac{\tilde\sigma}{\sigma_0}\,e^{2\tilde{s}^2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\sqrt{2}\,\tilde{s}\right),
  \qquad \tilde{s}\equiv\tilde\sigma/\tau_L.
  \label{eq:g2zero_voigt}
\end{equation}
For any Voigt profile with finite $\tau_L$, the parameter $\tilde{s} > 0$
and an O($s$) term is present, controlled by the Lorentzian component.
A purely Gaussian source ($\tau_L\to\infty$, $\tilde{s}\to 0$) has no O($s$)
correction: $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0)-1 = 1/\sqrt{1+2s^2(\tau_c/\tau_G)^2}$.

In the narrow-band limit $\sigma_\ell/(c\tau_c)\ll 1$
(Section~\ref{sec:narrowband}), $\sigma_0\ll\tau_c$ for any $\Phi_{\rm eff}$.
Substituting $u=\Delta/\sigma_0$ and expanding $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\sigma_0 u)-1$
for small $\sigma_0$, the convolution integral yields to first order in
$s=\sigma_0/\tau_c$:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0) - 1
  \approx \bigl[g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(0)-1\bigr]
          + \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\sigma_0\,g^{(2)\prime}_{\rm src}(0^+),
  \label{eq:g2zero_nb}
\end{equation}
where $g^{(2)\prime}_{\rm src}(0^+)$ denotes the right-derivative of $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$
at zero lag (the function is non-differentiable at $\tau=0$ for cusped profiles such
as the Lorentzian, so the symmetric derivative does not exist there).
For sources with a smooth (even) profile the right-derivative vanishes and the
leading correction is $O(\sigma_0^2)$; for a Lorentzian,
$g^{(2)\prime}_{\rm src}(0^+) = -2/\tau_L$, recovering
$\delta g^{(2)}\approx 2\sqrt{2/\pi}\,s$, consistent with the small-$s$
expansion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:deltag2}.
For a Voigt profile, the same expansion gives
$\delta g^{(2)} \approx 2\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sigma_0/\tau_L + (\sigma_0/\tau_G)^2 + \cdots$:
the O($s$) term is controlled solely by the Lorentzian component $\tau_L$;
maximizing foam sensitivity therefore favours spectrally resolved emission lines
with a Lorentzian component, since an unresolved line or a pure continuum erases
the cusp and reverts to O($s^2$).

The suppression $\delta g^{(2)}$ arises from a purely spatial mechanism with no
analogue in the point-source case: even at $\tau=0$, photon pairs from different
sky directions $\boldsymbol{\theta}\neq\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ accumulate independent
instantaneous jitters $\delta t(t;\boldsymbol{\theta})\neq\delta t(t;\boldsymbol{\theta}')$
when $b\gg r_\perp$, and their relative offset blurs the $g^{(2)}(0)$ measurement.
Because $|g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(0)|^2=1$ exactly is unaffected by this spatial jitter,
the two observables are controlled by different mechanisms and $\mathcal{S}(0)<0$.
This does not violate any fundamental inequality: the bound $\mathcal{S}\geq 0$
established in Section~\ref{sec:siegert} applies to a single ray, and the
angular mixture of an extended source need not satisfy it.

For the region $\alpha > 1/2$ that experiments seek to probe, $\sigma_\phi \ll 1$
and the sensitivity parameter
$s = \sigma_0/\tau_c
 = \sqrt{2(1-\Phi_{\rm eff})}\,\sigma_\phi\cdot(\Delta\lambda/\lambda)$
is small across all realistic observational bandwidths.
For the holographic model ($\alpha = 2/3$), $\sigma_\phi \sim 10^{-8}$, so
that even at a high spectral resolution
$R = \lambda/\Delta\lambda \sim 10^4$, one finds $s \sim 10^{-12}$.
In this regime the linear approximation $\delta g^{(2)} \approx 2\sqrt{2/\pi}\,s$
is essentially exact.

Beyond raw detectability, the coherence observables serve as discriminants
against conventional propagation phase screens~\cite{Carlip2023}.
Furthermore, for foam models with long-range transverse correlations image
blurring is strongly suppressed~\cite{LeeZurekChen2024}, making the coherence
observables developed here potentially the only channel sensitive to such models.
The source-size dependence of $\delta g^{(2)}$ through $\Phi_{\rm eff}$ provides
a direct probe of $r_\perp$ with no analogue in a featureless propagation screen,
which carries no transverse coherence structure.
The mathematical structure of the foam suppression factor $\mathcal{F}_{\rm foam}$
is identical to that of any Gaussian random phase screen, but two signatures
distinguish foam: distance scaling and achromaticity.
For spacetime foam $\sigma_\phi^2\propto D^{2(1-\alpha)}$ ($D^1$ for $\alpha=1/2$,
$D^{2/3}$ for $\alpha=2/3$, $D^0$ for $\alpha=1$), whereas a thin intervening
screen scales as $D$ and Kolmogorov turbulence as $D^{11/6}$; the holographic
and conservative foam models predict sub-linear or flat growth qualitatively
different from any known astrophysical screen.
Geometrically, foam jitter ($\delta t=\Delta\ell/c$) is achromatic ---
independent of $\lambda$ --- in contrast to plasma-based effects which scale
as $\lambda^2$.
The Fermi, H.E.S.S., and LHAASO time-of-flight analyses~\cite{Fermi2009,HESS2008,LHAASO2024} rule
out deterministic, energy-ordered LIV dispersion as a class of quantum-gravity
effect, but do not constrain the stochastic achromatic foam jitter considered
here (Section~\ref{sec:intro}); the principal chromatic alternative that the
achromaticity discriminant addresses is therefore plasma propagation.
In practice, the intrinsic source visibility $V_{\rm src}(\tau)$ introduces a
degeneracy with foam suppression, but the Siegert discrepancy
$\mathcal{S}(\tau) = g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau) - 1 - |g^{(1)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)|^2$
is constructed directly from the data: a detection of $\mathcal{S}\neq 0$
requires no explicit model of the source spectrum, since $g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)$
can be obtained from the source spectrum via the Wiener--Khinchin theorem.

% ==============================================================
\section{Siegert-Violation: Sensitivity and Target Selection}
\label{sec:prospects}
% ==============================================================

This section examines the detectability of Siegert violation via the HBT effect
and surveys candidate astrophysical sources: broad-line AGN and quasars,
Galactic and Local-Group transients, and nearby stars.
We use the Hanbury Brown--Twiss (HBT) split-beam configuration~\cite{HBT1956}
throughout, as essentially all modern photon-counting $g^{(2)}$ measurements
do: detector dead time makes a single-detector autocorrelation blind to the
ps-scale lags that build the bunching peak.

The sensitivity estimates that follow assume a Lorentzian line profile, adopted
as the default for the broad and narrow permitted lines considered here.
Two variants are considered: temporal decorrelation at finite lag and transverse
decorrelation at zero lag.
The pure-Lorentzian cusp in $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$ at $\tau=0$ maximizes the
linear-in-$s$ scaling of Eq.~\eqref{eq:deltag2}; any departure from a pure
Lorentzian rounds the cusp and reverts the extended-source discrepancy to
$O(s^2)$, so the SNR estimates for that variant should be read as optimistic
limits.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Aperture Scaling}

The HBT observable is $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(\tau)$, whose foam-free value for a thermal
source equals 2 at $\tau=0$ and decays to 1 on the source coherence timescale.
Foam imprints two distinct signatures on this observable: a Siegert discrepancy
at finite lag (Section~\ref{sec:siegert}), applicable to both point and extended
sources, and a suppression of the zero-lag bunching peak specific to spatially
extended sources (Section~\ref{sec:extended}).
Both are inferred by comparing the measured $g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}$ to the Siegert
prediction $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}-1=|g^{(1)}_{\rm src}|^2$, built from the source
spectrum via the Wiener--Khinchin theorem; neither requires a separate model of
the source.

The photon-counting rate is $R = \epsilon\, A\, \dot{n}$, where $\dot{n}$ is the
source photon flux per unit area within the filter bandpass, $\epsilon$ the
end-to-end throughput, and $A$ the collecting area. We adopt the baseline
instrumental configuration of Kim \emph{et al.}~\cite{Kim2025}: telescope
diameter $d=9.96\,$m ($A=77.9\,$m$^2$), mirror reflectivity 0.73, filter
transmission 0.90, detector quantum efficiency 0.60, total throughput
$\epsilon=0.39$, and timing resolution FWHM $=30\,$ps ($\sigma_t=13\,$ps rms).
The astrophysical targets of interest have intrinsic coherence times
$\tau_L \lesssim 1$\,ps, so $\sigma_t \gg \tau_L$ for the baseline instrument and
the measurement sits firmly in the detector-limited regime: an unresolved
foam feature of width $\tau_L$ is diluted to
$(\tau_L/\sigma_t)\,|\mathcal{S}|$ in a time bin of width $\sigma_t$, with
statistical noise
$\sigma[g^{(2)}_{\rm meas}]=1/(R\sqrt{\sigma_t T_{\rm obs}})$. We present the
detector-limited SNR as the primary case throughout and quote the source-limited
limit ($\tau_L \gtrsim \sigma_t$, requiring sub-ps timing) only as the gain
that next-generation detectors would unlock with precision optics.

\subsubsection{Temporal Decorrelation -- Finite-lag variant}
\label{sec:sens-point}

For a point-like source,
the foam signature manifests as a
Siegert discrepancy at finite lag $\tau \ne 0$.
Considering a Lorentzian source at the dynamical limit
$C_\alpha(\tau_L;D)\to 0$, the discrepancy at $\tau \sim \tau_L$ is given
by Eq.~\ref{eq:Stc}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}(\tau_L) \;=\; e^{-2}\!\left(1 - e^{-2\sigma_\phi^2}\right),
\label{eq:siegert_pt_repeat}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\phi = 2\pi\sigma_\ell(D)/\lambda$ is the rms accumulated phase
(Eq.~\ref{eq:sigmaphi}). The signal is \emph{quadratic} in $\sigma_\phi$ in the
weak-foam limit, $\mathcal{S}(\tau_L)\approx 2e^{-2}\sigma_\phi^2$, and saturates
at $e^{-2}\approx 0.135$ for $\sigma_\phi\gg 1$.

\paragraph{Detector-limited SNR.}
With $\sigma_t \gg \tau_L$ for all sources of interest, the bunching feature
of width $\tau_L$ is unresolved in a time bin of width $\sigma_t$, and
\begin{equation}
\textrm{SNR}_{\rm det}^{\rm pt}
\;=\; \frac{\tau_L}{\sigma_t}|\mathcal{S}(\tau_L)|\cdot R\sqrt{\sigma_t T_{\rm obs}}
\;\xrightarrow{\sigma_\phi\ll 1}\;
2e^{-2}\sigma_\phi^2\, R\,\frac{\tau_L}{\sqrt{\sigma_t}}\sqrt{T_{\rm obs}}.
\label{eq:snr_det_pt}
\end{equation}
The detector-limited form carries a $\sqrt{\tau_L/\sigma_t}$ penalty relative
to source-limited operation
($\textrm{SNR}_{\rm src}^{\rm pt} = |\mathcal{S}(\tau_L)|\,R\sqrt{\tau_L T_{\rm obs}}$);
reaching the source-limited limit on broad-line quasars requires
$\sigma_t \lesssim \tau_L \sim 0.1$\,ps.

\paragraph{Filter strategy.}
Since $\mathcal{S}(\tau_L)$ depends only on $\sigma_\phi$, the filter
choice affects the SNR only through $R\,\tau_L$; filtering narrower than
the intrinsic linewidth simply replaces $\tau_L$ with the post-filter
coherence time $\tau_c = \lambda^2/(c\,\Delta\lambda_{\rm filter})$.
In the detector-limited regime the SNR scales as $R\,\tau_L/\sqrt{\sigma_t}$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_pt}); for a Lorentzian emission line the transmitted
fraction scales as $\Delta\lambda_{\rm filter}/\Delta\lambda_{\rm line}$ in the
narrow-filter limit, so $R\,\tau_L$ is approximately constant and the SNR is
insensitive to filter width.
The same holds for a flat continuum source where
$R\propto\Delta\lambda_{\rm filter}$ and
$\tau_c\propto\Delta\lambda_{\rm filter}^{-1}$.
The practical bound on filter width is set by sky background: widening the
filter beyond the emission line adds sky photons without increasing source
signal, degrading the signal-to-background ratio.

\paragraph{Distance and aperture scaling.}
Substituting $R\propto D^{-2}$ at fixed luminosity and
$\sigma_\phi^2\propto D^{2(1-\alpha)}$ into Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_pt} gives, in
the weak-foam limit,
\begin{equation}
\textrm{SNR}^{\rm pt} \;\propto\; D^{2(1-\alpha)}\cdot D^{-2} \;=\; D^{-2\alpha},
\label{eq:dist_scaling_pt}
\end{equation}
saturating at $\textrm{SNR}\propto D^{-2}$ in the strong-foam limit.
Since $\alpha > 0$, the SNR increases toward smaller $D$: nearby targets are preferred.

The minimum detectable path-length amplitude follows from inverting
Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_pt} with the quadratic signal dependence
$|\mathcal{S}(\tau_L)|\propto\sigma_\phi^2\propto\sigma_\ell^2$:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\ell,\rm min}^{\rm pt} \;\propto\;
\frac{\lambda}{d}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_t}{\tau_L^2\, T_{\rm obs}}\right)^{\!1/4},
\label{eq:sigma_ell_min_pt}
\end{equation}
where $R\propto d^2$ has been used. Aperture scales as $d^{-1}$ rather than
$d^{-2}$, and observing time as $T_{\rm obs}^{-1/4}$ rather than
$T_{\rm obs}^{-1/2}$ --- both costs of the quadratic dependence on
$\sigma_\phi$. Each additional decade of sensitivity in $\sigma_\ell$ requires
four decades of integration time, compared with two for the extended-source
variant.

\subsubsection{Transverse Decorrelation -- Zero-lag variant}
\label{sec:sens-extended}

For a spatially extended source whose angular size satisfies
$D\Delta\theta\gtrsim r_\perp$ (equivalently $\Phi_{\rm eff}\to 0$), photon
pairs from different sky directions accumulate independent foam jitters and
the zero-lag bunching peak is suppressed below its canonical value of 2:
\begin{equation}
g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0) \;=\; 1 + e^{2s^2}\,\textrm{erfc}\!\left(\sqrt{2}\,s\right),
\qquad s \equiv \sigma_0/\tau_L,
\label{eq:g2_zero_repeat}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_0^2 = 2\sigma_\ell^2(D)(1-\Phi_{\rm eff})/c^2$
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:g2zero}--\ref{eq:sigma0}). The foam signal is the zero-lag
Siegert discrepancy
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}(0) \;=\; g^{(2)}_{\rm obs}(0) - 2 \;\le\; 0,
\label{eq:sneg_repeat}
\end{equation}
a deficit relative to the Siegert prediction $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(0)=2$. In the
weak-foam limit $s\ll 1$ relevant to all viable foam models,
$|\mathcal{S}(0)| = \delta g^{(2)} \approx 2\sqrt{2/\pi}\,s$ scales
\emph{linearly} in $s$ --- a consequence of the cusp in $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$ at
$\tau=0$ for sources with a Lorentzian line component (Eq.~\ref{eq:deltag2},
Section~\ref{sec:zerolag}). The signal saturates at
$|\mathcal{S}(0)|\to 1$ for $s\gg 1$.

Two structural conditions distinguish this variant from the finite-lag case
and govern its applicability.

\paragraph{Source-size requirement.}
The candidate targets are physically large and thus extended sources, even
though they are difficult to resolve from Earth; as extended sources, they are
sensitive to spacetime foam whose transverse coherence length satisfies
$r_\perp \lesssim D\,\Delta\theta$ (equivalently $\Phi_{\rm eff} \approx 0$),
through the cross-ray differential jitter mechanism.
The mechanism requires $\Phi_{\rm eff}<1$, equivalently
$D\Delta\theta\gtrsim r_\perp$: rays from different sky directions must sample
distinct transverse regions of the foam to accumulate independent jitters. For
sources unresolved on the foam transverse scale ($\Phi_{\rm eff}\to 1$),
$\sigma_0\to 0$ and the variant becomes inactive; the finite-lag variant of
Section~\ref{sec:sens-point} then provides the only HBT channel. Because
$r_\perp$ is itself an unknown parameter of the foam model, the source-size
dependence of $\delta g^{(2)}$ across a sample of targets with varying
$D\Delta\theta$ is the principal observational handle on $r_\perp$
(Section~\ref{sec:extended}).

\paragraph{Filter strategy: preserving the intrinsic cusp.}
The linear-in-$s$ scaling of $\delta g^{(2)}$ requires that the filter pass
the full emission line,
\begin{equation}
\Delta\lambda_{\rm filter} \;\ge\; \textrm{FWHM}_{\rm line},
\label{eq:filter_cusp}
\end{equation}
so that the post-filter coherence time is set by the intrinsic Lorentzian line
width rather than the (smooth) filter profile; a narrower filter destroys the
cusp in $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$ at zero lag and reverts $\delta g^{(2)}$ to $O(s^2)$.

\paragraph{Detector-limited SNR.}
With $\sigma_t \gg \tau_L$ for all sources of interest, the bunching peak of
width $\sim\tau_L$ is unresolved: the measured zero-lag excess in a time bin
of width $\sigma_t$ is diluted to
$|\mathcal{S}(0)|_{\rm meas}=(\tau_L/\sigma_t)|\mathcal{S}(0)|$, with
statistical noise
$\sigma[g^{(2)}_{\rm meas}]=1/(R\sqrt{\sigma_t T_{\rm obs}})$~\cite{Foellmi2009,
Dravins2013, Guerin2017}, so
\begin{equation}
\textrm{SNR}_{\rm det}^{\rm ext}
\;=\; \frac{\tau_L}{\sigma_t}|\mathcal{S}(0)|\cdot R\sqrt{\sigma_t T_{\rm obs}}
\;\xrightarrow{s\ll 1}\;
2\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\sigma_0\, R\sqrt{T_{\rm obs}/\sigma_t}.
\label{eq:snr_det_ext}
\end{equation}
The SNR in this regime is independent of $\tau_L$: the cusp signal $\propto
1/\tau_L$ is cancelled by the $\tau_L/\sigma_t$ dilution factor. The penalty
relative to source-limited operation
($\textrm{SNR}_{\rm src}^{\rm ext} = |\mathcal{S}(0)|\,R\sqrt{\tau_L T_{\rm obs}}$,
attainable only when $\sigma_t \lesssim \tau_L$) is $\sqrt{\tau_L/\sigma_t}$.

\paragraph{Distance and aperture scaling.}
Substituting $R\propto D^{-2}$ at fixed luminosity and
$\sigma_0\propto\sigma_\ell(D)\propto D^{1-\alpha}$ into Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_ext}
gives, in the weak-foam limit,
\begin{equation}
\textrm{SNR}^{\rm ext} \;\propto\; D^{1-\alpha}\cdot D^{-2} \;=\; D^{-(1+\alpha)},
\label{eq:dist_scaling_ext}
\end{equation}
with the penalty steeper for larger $\alpha$ ($D^{-3/2}$ for $\alpha=1/2$,
$D^{-5/3}$ for $\alpha=2/3$). In the strong-foam limit $|\mathcal{S}(0)|\to 1$
saturates and $\textrm{SNR}\propto D^{-2}$. A nearby source of modest
luminosity but high apparent flux can therefore be competitive with a more
luminous but distant source: the higher photon rate $R$ at close range can
compensate for the reduced foam signal $|\mathcal{S}(0)|$.

The minimum detectable path-length amplitude follows from inverting
Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_ext} with the linear signal dependence
$|\mathcal{S}(0)|\propto\sigma_0\propto\sigma_\ell$:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\ell,\rm min}^{\rm ext} \;\propto\;
\frac{\sigma_t}{d^2\sqrt{\tau_L T_{\rm obs}}},
\label{eq:sigma_ell_min_ext}
\end{equation}
where $R\propto d^2$ has been used: sensitivity improves linearly with
$\sigma_t$ in the detector-limited regime and scales as $d^{-2}$ (collecting
area) and as $T_{\rm obs}^{-1/2}$, both more favourable than the corresponding
$d^{-1}$ and $T_{\rm obs}^{-1/4}$ scalings of the finite-lag variant.
Importantly, this variant is independent of the foam temporal correlation
$C_\alpha$: the cross-ray differential jitter that drives $\delta g^{(2)}(0)$
operates regardless of whether the foam is dynamically active or quasi-static,
in contrast to the finite-lag variant which requires
$\tau_{\rm foam}\lesssim\tau_L$.

\subsection{Candidate Sources}
\label{sec:candidate_sources}

Two source properties drive target selection for both variants: the photon rate
$R$, set by the total line flux captured within the filter, and the source
distance $D$, which sets $\sigma_\phi$ (finite-lag, Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_pt})
and $\sigma_0$ (zero-lag, Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_ext}).
For the zero-lag observable a third property matters: the line profile must
retain a Lorentzian core to preserve the cusp in $g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$ at zero
lag and hence the linear-in-$s$ scaling (Section~\ref{sec:zerolag}); narrower
lines (shorter $\tau_L$) yield a larger cusp parameter $s = \sigma_0/\tau_L$
per unit foam amplitude and are therefore preferred targets at fixed photon rate.
The finite-lag observable imposes no line-profile constraint and is applicable
to any spatially unresolved source.
All sources discussed below have intrinsic coherence times $\tau_L \lesssim 1$\,ps,
well below the baseline timing resolution $\sigma_t = 13$\,ps; the measurements
therefore operate in the detector-limited regime throughout.
The source classes surveyed below are illustrative rather than exhaustive;
systematic searches through existing AGN flux catalogues and emission-line
surveys will likely identify superior targets with higher photon rates,
more purely Lorentzian line profiles, or more favorable distance-to-flux ratios.
Observed properties and references for the principal candidates are collected
in Tables~\ref{tab:source_agn} and~\ref{tab:source_etacar}.

\textit{Broad-line quasars.}
Luminous broad-line quasars (BLQs) combine large cosmological distances ---
maximising both $\sigma_\phi$ (finite-lag) and $\sigma_0$ (zero-lag) --- with
high emission-line flux.
Their broad permitted lines (H$\alpha$, H$\beta$, Mg\,\textsc{ii}, C\,\textsc{iv})
are empirically well described by Lorentzian profiles~\cite{Kollatschny2013},
satisfying the zero-lag cusp requirement of Section~\ref{sec:candidate_sources}.
A wide filter ($\Delta\lambda_{\rm filter} \geq \mathrm{FWHM}$) captures the full
line flux, giving photon rates far exceeding those of a continuum source at the
same apparent magnitude; sky background and detector dark counts are negligible
relative to the source rate, so the Brouri~et~al.\ correction for accidental
coincidences is small~\cite{Brouri2000}.
We adopt 3C~273 (broad H$\alpha$, $F \approx 2\times10^{-12}$\,erg\,s$^{-1}$\,cm$^{-2}$,
FWHM $\approx 127$\,\AA, $D = 677$\,Mpc) as the reference target~\cite{Boroson1992}.
With a $\sim 200$\,\AA\ filter (capturing $\approx 70\%$ of the Lorentzian line flux),
the photon rate on a 10\,m telescope with $\epsilon = 0.39$ is $R \approx 1.4\times10^5$\,cps
and the intrinsic coherence time is $\tau_L \approx 0.11$\,ps, placing the measurement
deeply in the detector-limited regime ($\tau_L \ll \sigma_t = 13$\,ps).

\textit{Narrow-line Seyfert 1 galaxies.}
Narrow-line Seyfert 1 galaxies (NLS1s) have permitted lines with FWHM $\lesssim 2000$\,km\,s$^{-1}$,
empirically well described by a Lorentzian core~\cite{Kollatschny2013}.
A narrower line yields a shorter intrinsic coherence time $\tau_L$ and hence a
larger cusp parameter $s = \sigma_0/\tau_L$ per unit foam amplitude:
for FWHM $\sim 1300$\,km\,s$^{-1}$ ($\sim 28$\,\AA\ at H$\alpha$),
$\tau_L \approx 0.51$\,ps, compared with $\tau_L \approx 0.11$\,ps for 3C~273.
Because NLS1 distances are smaller, their $\sigma_0$ is correspondingly smaller,
but the genuinely Lorentzian core and the high photon rates make them competitive
targets for the $\delta g^{(2)}$ measurement, and NLS1s with
$\tau_L \gtrsim \sigma_t$ can operate in the source-limited regime
with next-generation detectors.
Mrk~766 ($V = 13.6$, $D = 53$\,Mpc, FWHM $\approx 1300$\,km\,s$^{-1}$,
$F \approx 5\times10^{-13}$\,erg\,s$^{-1}$\,cm$^{-2}$, $\tau_L \approx 0.51$\,ps)
is a well-studied example~\cite{Kollatschny2013}.

\textit{Galactic emission-line sources as calibrators.}
Bright Galactic sources --- Wolf-Rayet stars (e.g.\ $\gamma^2$\,Vel, WR\,6),
luminous blue variables, and symbiotic binaries --- can provide high photon rates
with genuinely Lorentzian line cores from pressure or electron-scattering broadening.

\textit{Galactic and Local-Group transients.}
Type~IIn supernovae and LBV giant eruptions exhibit strong narrow H$\alpha$
emission throughout their bright phase from circumstellar-material interaction,
with intrinsic coherence times $\tau_L \sim 0.3$--$1$\,ps that, with
next-generation detectors ($\sigma_t \lesssim \tau_L$), enter the
source-limited regime.
A representative SN~IIn line has FWHM $\approx 55$\,\AA\
($\tau_L \approx 0.26$\,ps); the nearby LBV $\eta$\,Car ($D \approx 2.3$\,kpc,
FWHM $\approx 15$\,\AA, $\tau_L \approx 0.95$\,ps) reached $m_V \approx -1$
during its 1840s eruption --- briefly the brightest star in the sky.

Two caveats apply to $\eta$\,Car.
The Homunculus Nebula scatters substantial flux into the line of sight, so the
observed coherence is partially set by scattering geometry rather than the
central engine; this must be modeled to credit the photon count correctly.
During an eruption the photospheric envelope reaches ${\sim}2$\,mas at
$2.3$\,kpc within ${\sim}10$\,yr at $v \sim 500$\,km\,s$^{-1}$, resolved by
intensity-interferometry baselines $\gtrsim 50$\,m; the source is therefore
no longer a clean point for the Section~\ref{sec:siegert} Siegert test but
does cleanly support the Section~\ref{sec:extended} extended-source
$\delta g^{(2)}$ test, with a directly imageable angular structure to anchor
$\Phi_{\rm eff}$.

\subsection{SNR Results}
\label{sec:snr_results}

SNR as a function of foam exponent $\alpha$ for the sources above is shown in
Figs.~\ref{fig:snr_vs_alpha_ext} and~\ref{fig:snr_vs_alpha_pt} for the zero-lag
and finite-lag variants respectively.

\paragraph{Current- and next-generation sensitivity.}
With current instrumentation ($\sigma_t=13\,$ps), the broad-line quasar
3C\,273 ($\tau_L\approx 0.11\,$ps) operates deeply in the detector-limited
regime. From Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_ext} at $3\sigma$
($R\approx 1.4\times 10^5\,$cps, $T_{\rm obs}=10^4\,$hr):
$\sigma_{\ell,\rm min}\approx 1.7\,\mu$m. This is far above the Lieu--Hillman
imaging floor ($\sigma_\ell\approx 105\,$nm at H$\alpha$), reflecting the
intrinsic weakness of the envelope-timing observable $\delta g^{(2)}(0)$
relative to the carrier-phase observable $\sigma_\phi=2\pi\sigma_\ell/\lambda$
that governs fringe visibility.

The detector-limited penalty $\sqrt{\tau_L/\sigma_t}$ vanishes only when
$\sigma_t$ is reduced below $\tau_L$, which for broad-line quasars demands
sub-0.1\,ps timing. Superconducting nanowire detectors achieving such jitter
are under active development; at $\sigma_t=0.05\,$ps, broad-line quasars enter
the source-limited regime and NLS1s ($\tau_L\sim 0.5\,$ps) are deeply
source-limited, giving $\sigma_{\ell,\rm min}\approx 160\,$nm (10\,m),
$10\,$nm (40\,m), $0.02\,$nm (1\,km array) on 3C\,273. A 40\,m-class
telescope with sub-ps timing would reach well below the Lieu--Hillman floor
via $\delta g^{(2)}(0)$ alone, and a kilometre-baseline intensity-interferometer
array would achieve sub-nanometre sensitivity --- placing the random-walk
model $\alpha=1/2$ with long-range transverse
correlations~\cite{LeeZurekChen2024} firmly within reach across the full
AGN/quasar redshift range.

\paragraph{Comparison of the two variants.}
For the 3C\,273 reference parameters
($R\approx 1.4\times 10^5\,$cps, $\tau_L\approx 0.11\,$ps, $T_{\rm obs}=10^4\,$hr,
$\lambda=\mathrm{H}\alpha$, $D=677\,$Mpc), the random-walk model $\alpha=1/2$
with short-range transverse correlations relaxed gives $\sigma_\phi\approx 230$,
saturating the finite-lag signal at $\mathcal{S}(\tau_L)\to e^{-2}$ and
$\sigma_{\ell,\rm min}^{\rm pt}\sim 10^2\,$nm --- comparable to the
Lieu--Hillman fringe-erasure threshold, consistent with the finite-lag
observable accessing $\sigma_\phi$ directly.
For the holographic model $\alpha=2/3$, $\sigma_\phi\sim 10^{-8}$ gives
$\mathcal{S}(\tau_L)\sim 10^{-16}$, five orders of magnitude below the
zero-lag signal $\delta g^{(2)}(0)\sim 10^{-11}$ on the same target.
The finite-lag variant is therefore not competitive with the zero-lag
variant for raw sensitivity to the holographic model, but it provides the only
HBT channel sensitive to the foam temporal dynamics $\tau_{\rm foam}$ and can
be applied to spatially unresolved sources for which the zero-lag
mechanism is inactive.

For a galactic transient at $D \sim 2$\,kpc with $\alpha = 1/2$ and long
transverse correlations, the predicted $\sigma_\ell \approx 30$\,nm and the
photon flux is so high that detection or exclusion is essentially instantaneous
once sub-ps timing is available.
For $\alpha = 2/3$, a galactic SN~IIn or LBV eruption returns a
$10^3$--$10^4\times$ SNR improvement over the persistent-AGN baseline.
The galactic SN~IIn case yields the largest single-event SNR --- ${\sim}10^4$
times the 3C~273 baseline --- but its $\sim$millennial recurrence makes it
a ``drop-everything'' opportunity rather than a campaign target; the most
recent confirmed Galactic core-collapse supernova was Cassiopeia~A
(${\sim}1680$, Type~IIb).
The $\eta$\,Car case sacrifices a factor of $\sim 10$ in SNR but offers a
recurrence plausibly within a working career: $\eta$\,Car itself has been
in an unusual brightening state since the mid-twentieth century, and giant
LBV eruptions are now routinely identified in nearby galaxies
(SN\,2009ip and analogs).
For both galactic sources, the $\alpha = 1/2$ prediction with long-range
transverse correlations is within reach of the HBT framework: a near-instant
test of the long-$r_\perp$ random-walk variant.
The M31 case is intermediate: the $\alpha = 1/2$ short-$r_\perp$ prediction
($\sigma_\ell \approx 625$\,nm) is already incompatible with existing
imaging of M31, but the long-$r_\perp$ variant remains viable, and the
$\alpha = 2/3$ photon-flux advantage is real if modest.

\subsection{Sources Investigated but Not Competitive}
\label{sec:not_competitive}

\subsubsection{The Sun as a Continuum Source}
\label{sec:sun_continuum}

In the finite-lag variant, a continuum source whose spectrum is flat within
the bandpass has its coherence function set entirely by the filter: a filter
of width $\Delta\lambda$ gives $\tau_c = \lambda^2/(c\,\Delta\lambda)$, and
the narrow-band Siegert discrepancy (Eq.~\ref{eq:siegert_pt_repeat}) applies
with this filter-defined $\tau_c$.
The Sun, observed through a single spatial coherence cell, illustrates the
finite-lag point-source case at extreme proximity.
At $D = 1$\,AU and $\lambda = 500$\,nm,
$\sigma_\phi(\alpha=1/2) \approx 2\times10^{-5}$, giving
$\mathcal{S}(\tau_c) \approx 1.1\times10^{-10}$.
With a 1\,\AA\ filter ($\tau_c\approx 8$\,ps, comparable to $\sigma_t$), the
per-mode photon rate on a 10\,m telescope is $r\sim 10^8$\,cps ---
enormous compared to any extragalactic target.
The single-channel source-limited SNR is
$|\mathcal{S}|\,r\sqrt{\tau_c T_{\rm obs}} \sim 2\times10^{-4}$
for $T_{\rm obs}=10^4$\,hr.
Even multiplexing with a high-resolution spectrograph ($N\sim3000$ channels,
$\sqrt{N}\sim55$ gain) raises the SNR only to $\sim10^{-2}$, still far below
detection: the deficit is set entirely by the small
$\sigma_\phi^2\propto D^{2(1-\alpha)}$ at 1\,AU, which is $\sim 10^{16}$ times
smaller than at 1\,Gpc.

\subsubsection{Absorption-Line Sources}
\label{sec:absorption_lines}

The framework applies without modification to sources observed in absorption:
absorption is a linear spectral filter that preserves the Gaussian chaotic
character of the light, so the Siegert relation and its foam violation apply
exactly as before (Eq.~\ref{eq:Stc}).
For $\mathcal{S}(\tau_c)$ the relevant observable is $\sigma_\phi(D)$, which depends
only on the source distance and foam model, not on whether the source is observed in
emission or absorption.
Unlike a narrow emission line, which concentrates photons and enhances $R$ by the
inverse line-fraction factor, a broad absorption line can only reduce $R$ relative
to the unobscured continuum: there is no photon-rate advantage from the absorption
profile.

The practical consequence is illustrated by the best-case absorption-line target.
Sirius~B (DA white dwarf, $V = 8.44$, $D = 2.64$\,pc) has a deep, Stark-broadened
H$\alpha$ profile (FWHM $\sim 20$\,\AA, central depth $\sim 70\%$).
On a 10\,m telescope with a $1.1$\,\AA\ filter, the transmitted continuum rate
is $R_{\rm cont} \approx 8.8\times10^5$\,cps, reduced to
$R_{\rm abs} \approx 2.6\times10^5$\,cps at line center.
At $D = 2.64$\,pc and $\alpha = 1/2$, $\sigma_\phi \approx 0.011 \ll 1$, giving
$\mathcal{S}(\tau_c) \approx 3.3\times10^{-5}$ and
$\mathrm{SNR}/\sqrt{T/\mathrm{hr}} \approx 1.9\times10^{-3}$ ---
about $17\times$ smaller than a 3C~273-class AGN in the saturated regime
($\mathcal{S} \to e^{-2}$, $\mathrm{SNR}/\sqrt{T/\mathrm{hr}} \approx 3.3\times10^{-2}$).
For $\alpha = 2/3$, $\sigma_\phi \approx 2.7\times10^{-11}$ at 2.64\,pc:
the foam signal is entirely negligible.
Absorption-line sources are therefore not competitive as foam probes; their value
lies in null-test calibration, where a nearby DA white dwarf provides a
Lorentzian-profile source with negligible predicted foam signal for any plausible~$\alpha$.

The solar Ca\,\textsc{ii}\,K line ($\lambda = 3934$\,\AA) illustrates the combined
limits of proximity and extended-source dilution.
The photospheric damping wings are pressure-broadened with a Lorentzian FWHM
$\Gamma_{\rm abs} \approx 0.3$\,\AA, but the Lorentzian wing absorption at
$\Delta\lambda \sim 2$\,\AA\ from line center is only $\sim 0.6\%$; filters placed
in the damping wing are observing essentially pure continuum with negligible
line-profile modulation.
The Sun is also an extended source: a 10\,m aperture collects
$M = A_{\rm tel}/A_{\rm coh} \approx 4\times10^{10}$ independent spatial modes
(coherence area $A_{\rm coh} = (\lambda/\theta_\odot)^2 \approx 1.8\times10^{-9}$\,m$^2$
for angular diameter $\theta_\odot \approx 9.3$\,mrad), each contributing an independent
foam phase.
The SNR formula (Eq.~\ref{eq:snr_det_ext}) therefore applies with the per-mode rate
$r = R_{\rm total}/M = 2\bar{n}(\lambda)\,\Delta\nu\,\epsilon$
(Section~\ref{sec:extended}), where
$\bar{n}(\lambda_K) = [e^{hc/\lambda_K k_B T}-1]^{-1} \approx 1.8\times10^{-3}$
for $T=5778$\,K, giving $r \approx 1.1\times10^8$\,cps
($\Delta\nu = \sigma_t^{-1}$, two polarisations).
Despite this, the foam signal is minuscule:
$\sigma_\phi(1\,\mathrm{AU},\,\lambda_K,\,\alpha=1/2) \approx 2.5\times10^{-5}$, giving
$\mathcal{S}(\tau_c) \approx 1.7\times10^{-10}$ and
$\mathrm{SNR}/\sqrt{T/\mathrm{hr}} \approx 2\times10^{-6}$,
about $10^4\times$ below the AGN reference.
The root cause is proximity: $\sigma_\phi \propto D^{1/2-\alpha}$ at $D = 1$\,AU
is $\sim 10^8$ times smaller than at $D = 1$\,Gpc, and no photon-rate advantage
can compensate.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have developed a theoretical framework for the imprint of spacetime foam, characterized 
by the foam exponent $\alpha$, on both first- and second-order optical coherence. The 
central finding is that foam-induced arrival-time jitter produces two classes of observable
violation of relations that hold for all Gaussian chaotic sources: a deviation from the
Siegert relation, $g^{(2)}(\tau) - 1 = |g^{(1)}(\tau)|^2$, which requires the foam to be
dynamically active ($\tau_{\rm foam}$ short relative to the optical coherence time), and a
suppression of the zero-lag bunching peak below its canonical value of $g^{(2)}(0) = 2$,
which depends only on $\sigma_\ell^2(D)$ and is independent of $\tau_{\rm foam}$. These second-order observables 
are complementary to the fringe visibility and Strehl ratio tests based on first-order 
coherence that have already been applied to constrain foam models.
For the holographic model ($\alpha = 2/3$), all such observables lie well within the
currently inaccessible parameter space: the first-order tests probe effects at the
${\sim}10^{-8}$ level, while the second-order observables are characteristically smaller
at ${\sim}10^{-11}$ (a recent analysis of GRB\,221009A claims consistency with holographic
foam blurring~\cite{SteinbringNg2026}, though without formal statistical significance).
For the holographic model the predicted signals at ${\sim}10^{-11}$ lie well
below the sensitivity of current and near-term instrumentation; foam models
with $1/2 \ll  \alpha < 2/3$ predict larger signals and may be within reach.

The advantage of the second-order approach lies not in raw sensitivity but in two distinct
features.
First, the Siegert discrepancy $\mathcal{S}(\tau) = g^{(2)}(\tau)-1-|g^{(1)}(\tau)|^2$
is constructed directly from the data; a detection of $\mathcal{S}\neq 0$
can use the measured source spectrum to obtain $g^{(1)}_{\rm src}(\tau)$
via the Wiener--Khinchin theorem, without a separate source model.
Second, for extended sources whose line profile has a Lorentzian component
(natural or pressure broadening), the zero-lag bunching suppression $\delta g^{(2)}$
scales linearly with $s = \sigma_0/\tau_L$ --- a consequence of the cusp in
$g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$ at zero lag --- so that despite being a second-order coherence
measurement, $\delta g^{(2)}$ is not doubly suppressed relative to the first-order
observables.
Physically, the $\delta g^{(2)}$ suppression reflects the two-photon character of HBT
bunching: the zero-lag peak is built from photon pairs arriving from different sky
directions $\boldsymbol{\theta}\neq\boldsymbol{\theta}'$, which propagate through
distinct transverse regions of the foam and accumulate independent jitters
$\delta t(t;\boldsymbol{\theta})\neq\delta t(t;\boldsymbol{\theta}')$ when
$b\gg r_\perp$; their relative offset blurs $g^{(2)}(0)$ below its canonical value
of 2 (cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:zerolag}).

For the fiducial parameters $D = 1$~Gpc, $\lambda = 500$~nm,
$\Delta\lambda/\lambda = 10^{-3}$, $A_\alpha = 1$: the $\alpha = 1/2$ case gives
$\sigma_\phi \approx 280$ (fringes completely erased) and is excluded by
fringe-visibility measurements~\cite{Perlman2015,Christiansen2011,Ragazzoni2003}
under the standard assumption of short-range transverse foam correlations;
long-range-correlated random-walk foam~\cite{LeeZurekChen2024} evades this
bound and remains a target of the present framework.
The holographic case $\alpha = 2/3$ gives $\sigma_\phi \sim 10^{-8}$ --- six
orders of magnitude below the $\sigma_\phi \sim 1$ imaging exclusion ---
predicting $\mathcal{S}/|g^{(1)}_{\rm src}|^2 \sim 10^{-15}$ for the
finite-lag Siegert violation and $\delta g^{(2)} \approx 2\sqrt{2/\pi}\,s \sim 5.7\times 10^{-11}$
for the extended-source bunching suppression in the fully resolved limit
$\Phi_{\rm eff} \to 0$, where $s = \sqrt{2}\,\sigma_\phi\,(\Delta\lambda/\lambda) \sim 3.5\times10^{-11}$
(with $\delta g^{(2)}$ inheriting a linear rather
than quadratic scaling in $s = \sigma_0/\tau_L$ from the cusp in
$g^{(2)}_{\rm src}$ at zero lag for sources with a Lorentzian line component).

The achievable sensitivity and its scaling with telescope aperture and source
redshift are analyzed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:prospects}.
The observable is the zero-lag bunching suppression $\delta g^{(2)}(0)$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:deltag2}, $\Phi_{\rm eff}\approx 0$), measured via the HBT
split-beam configuration using bright broad-line AGN and quasars observed
through wide filters that preserve the intrinsic Lorentzian line cusp.
The linear-in-$s$ scaling of $\delta g^{(2)}$ requires that the filter pass the
full emission line ($\Delta\lambda_{\rm filter} \geq \mathrm{FWHM}_{\rm line}$);
this places bright quasars ($\tau_L \sim 0.1$\,ps) in the detector-limited regime
with current timing resolution ($\sigma_t = 13$\,ps), with a
$\sqrt{\tau_L/\sigma_t} \approx 0.09$ penalty relative to source-limited operation.
For a 10\,m-class telescope~\cite{Kim2025} with $T_{\rm obs} = 10^4$\,hr on a
3C~273-class quasar, the current-detector sensitivity is
$\sigma_{\ell,\rm min} \approx 1.7\,\mu$m ($3\sigma$) --- above the
Lieu--Hillman imaging floor ($\sigma_\ell \approx 105$\,nm at H$\alpha$).
With next-generation timing ($\sigma_t \lesssim 0.1$\,ps, entering the
source-limited regime), a 40\,m aperture reaches
$\sigma_\ell \approx 10$\,nm, well below the imaging floor; a 1\,km-class
intensity-interferometer array would reach sub-nm sensitivity.

The astrophysical contribution of the framework developed here is threefold:
it closes the imaging-test loophole for foam models with long-range transverse
correlations, it provides a wavelength-dependence discriminant against chromatic
plasma scattering (the Siegert violation inherits the $\sigma_\phi \propto 1/\lambda$
scaling), and it enables $r_\perp$ inversion via $\delta g^{(2)}$ on nearby AGN
whose broad emission lines have Lorentzian components.
What HBT-on-AGN cannot do is reach the holographic prediction
$\sigma_\phi \sim 10^{-8}$, which lies six orders of magnitude below the most
aggressive HBT projection; closing this gap would require photon flux $\sim 10^{20}$
greater than from a luminous quasar.
The holographic regime is the natural target of tabletop holographic-noise
interferometers~\cite{VerlindeZurek2021} that bypass the photon-counting penalty.

The $g^{(2)}(0)$ observable developed here is already routinely computed in radio astronomy
under different names: spectral kurtosis~\cite{NitaGary2007,NitaGary2010}, used for
real-time RFI excision at the VLA, GBT, LOFAR, and CHIME, is constructed to estimate
$g^{(2)}(0)-1$ from channelized power samples (returning unity in expectation for a
stationary Gaussian field, up to finite-sample bias corrections), as does the modulation
index $m^2 \equiv \mathrm{Var}(I)/\langle I\rangle^2$, which equals $g^{(2)}(0)-1$
for stationary, noise-corrected intensity samples and is used to characterize single-pulse
intensity statistics of pulsars and FRBs~\cite{Weltevrede2006}.
The Siegert-violation framework developed here therefore translates directly to existing
radio data products.

Beyond persistent AGN campaigns, the framework identifies a class of
high-priority targets of opportunity: nearby high-luminosity transients ---
Type~IIn supernovae and LBV giant eruptions --- that offer $10^3$--$10^4\times$
the SNR of the persistent-AGN baseline for the same observing time
(Section~\ref{sec:snr_results}).
These events are rare and unpredictable, but their scientific yield is
exceptional: a galactic SN~IIn or an $\eta$\,Car-class eruption with
sub-ps timing available would provide an essentially instantaneous test of
the $\alpha = 1/2$ random-walk foam model with long-range transverse
correlations.
A standing rapid-response HBT plan --- covering $\eta$\,Car, any galactic
core-collapse supernova, and any nearby ($D \lesssim 30$\,Mpc) Type~IIn
identified by transient surveys --- would enable the framework to exploit
these high-yield events without requiring prior allocation.

\subsection*{Broad-line profile shapes}

The profile shape of the broad emission line determines the fraction of line
flux transmitted through a narrow filter and the functional form of
$g^{(2)}_{\rm src}(\tau)$ at zero lag (Section~\ref{sec:zerolag}).

Sulentic et al.~\cite{2002ApJ...566L..71S} and Zamfir et al.~\cite{2010MNRAS.403.1759Z}
establish an empirical division of AGN into two populations based on
FWHM(H$\beta$): \emph{Population~A} sources with FWHM $\lesssim 4000$\,km\,s$^{-1}$
have broad H$\beta$ profiles that are well described by a single Lorentzian,
while \emph{Population~B} sources with FWHM $\gtrsim 4000$\,km\,s$^{-1}$
require a double-Gaussian (a classical broad component plus a very
broad, redshifted component) and are not well described by a single Lorentzian.

Mrk~766 has FWHM $\approx 1300$\,km\,s$^{-1}$, placing it firmly in
Population~A; its broad H$\alpha$ profile is well described by a
Lorentzian~\cite{2002ApJ...566L..71S,2010MNRAS.403.1759Z}.

3C~273 has FWHM $\approx 6800$\,km\,s$^{-1}$ (rest-frame velocity equivalent of
the 127\,\AA\ observed-frame H$\beta$ width at $z = 0.158$~\cite{Boroson1992}),
placing it in Population~B.
Its broad H$\beta$ profile is not well described by a single Lorentzian and
instead requires a double-Gaussian decomposition~\cite{2002ApJ...566L..71S,2010MNRAS.403.1759Z}.
